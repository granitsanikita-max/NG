## Preface + Introduction
- Real subject of the book: **market-making**. Find the market's existing desire, measure it, then focus it onto your product. "People don't change: only the direction of their desires do."
- "Building larger mice, and then building terrifying fear of them" = shape the biggest market possible, intensify its reaction to the problem, and to your "exclusive" solution.
- **No formula works twice.** Every formula is the solution to a past problem; change one part of the problem and the formula breaks. Replace formulas with **analysis** = a set of diagnostic questions that let the problem dictate the answer.
- Copywriter = like a physicist/speculator: harnesses huge forces (millions of people's hopes, fears, desires) he didn't create and can't switch off.

## Ch 1 — Mass Desire
- **Copy cannot create desire.** It only channels desire that already exists onto a product. Fighting mass desire = death (Chrysler 1948 shorter car, Ford 1954 selling safety vs horsepower, Edsel vs compacts).
- **Mass desire = "the public spread of a private want."** A market is born when a private desire is shared by enough people to pay for advertising + profit.
- **Amplification Effect:** riding existing desire turns $1 into $50–100. Trying to *create* desire = education → at best $1 in for $1 out. Never educate the market from zero.
- **Two kinds of forces that create desire:**
  1. *Permanent forces* — mass instinct (attractiveness, virility, health) and mass technological problems (bad reception, rust, slow aspirin). Desire is obvious; job = distinguish your product, fresh appeal, stronger believability, shift desire from competitor's fulfillment to yours.
  2. *Forces of change* — trends/style (rising, peaking, reversing) and mass education (TV stars, media, gossip, all advertising combined). Job = timing: catch the tide early, know which appeal to stress now and when to switch.
- Copywriter's lifelong daily job: **detect, inventory and chart** the mass desires around him (force + direction).
- **3 stages of writing an ad:**
  1. **Choose the most powerful desire** for your product. Score each desire on 3 dimensions: **Urgency/intensity** (arthritis pain vs minor headache), **Staying power** (can't be satiated — hunger vs gourmet craving), **Scope** (how many people share it — saving gas vs preventing future repairs). Products touch 2–4 desires; only ONE can lead the headline. Wrong choice = nothing else matters.
  2. **Acknowledge / reinforce / offer the means to satisfy that desire in one headline statement**, starting at the prospect's awareness point (aware of product → start with product; aware only of desire → start with desire; only aware of a general problem → start with problem and crystallize it into a specific need).
  3. **Show how the product's performances inevitably satisfy that desire.**
- **Two products in every product:** physical (materials, construction) vs functional (what it does = what they pay for). **The physical product can never be the headline.** Physical facts are used later to: justify the price; document quality of the performance; prove the performance lasts; sharpen the mental picture of the performance (Rolls rubber-shielded chassis → silence); and **give the claim a fresh basis for believability** — a new mechanism, new quality, or freedom from old limitations ("point of difference" — often the copywriter suggests it to the manufacturer).
- **Method:** list every performance the product has → group each against the mass desire it satisfies → feature the ONE performance with the most sales power right now (car example: transportation, dependability, economy, power, recognition, value, novelty…).
- Headline gets one glance; only one thought penetrates. 1st thought → 2nd → 3rd → they read the ad. "Every product gives you dozens of keys. But only one will fit the lock." All other performances then just reinforce/document the main one.

## Ch 2 — State of Awareness
- Ad always **starts with the market and ends with the product**. Headline is about the market; may never mention the product.
- Headline is built from 3 questions: (1) mass desire, (2) **awareness** — how much they know about how your product satisfies it, (3) **sophistication** — how many similar products came before.
- **Headline's only job: stop the prospect and make him read the 2nd sentence.** Same job for every sentence after. Headline doesn't have to sell. Only for most-aware buyers + a price cut can the headline do the whole sale.
- Awareness = how close to the surface the desire is; aware of need only → aware it can be satisfied → aware it's in your product category → your product by name → at a given price.
- **More aware = easier sale, say less. Less aware = harder, needs more creativity.** Each stage separated by a "psychological wall": indifference on one side, intense interest on the other. A headline for one stage fails at another, and fails once the market moves on.

**1. Most Aware** — knows product, wants it, hasn't bought. Headline = product name + bargain price (e.g. "Formerly $149.50—Now Only $119.95"). The news is the price / free gift / fast delivery / nearby. Lowest-skill job.

**2. Knows product but doesn't yet want it** (most advertising lives here). Headline = product name (or big logo) + its superiority. 7 possible jobs:
  a) reinforce desire — via *association* ("Instrument of the Immortals"), *sensory sharpening* ("Tastes like you just picked it"), *illustration*
  b) sharpen image of HOW it satisfies (physical product/mechanism — Rolls clock; Zippo from a fish's belly)
  c) extend where/when it satisfies ("Thirst knows no season" — Coke in winter)
  d) new proof/documentation ("9 out of 10 screen stars…", boxer fails to flatten paper cup)
  e) announce a new mechanism that satisfies better (dog food that makes its own gravy)
  f) announce a new mechanism that removes former limitations ("no dusty odors"; hearing aid "inconspicuous beyond belief")
  g) completely change the image/mechanism to escape competition → this is sophistication (Ch 3)
  Body = elaborate the superiority: visualization, documentation, mechanization.

**3. Knows the desire, doesn't know a product exists (new products).** Two jobs: pinpoint the still-vague desire spreading through the market, then crystallize desire + solution so sharply everyone recognizes it at a glance. **3 steps: name the desire and/or its solution in the headline → prove it can be done → show the mechanism is in your product.** From here on, execution > mechanics; this is the "idea man's" domain. Needs: analysis, intuition (spot a trend early), verbal creativity (give a name to the still-undefined, create a catchword).
  Examples: "Who else wants a whiter wash—with no hard work?", "How to win friends and influence people", "Now! Run your car without spark plugs", "Whoever heard of 17,000 blooms from a single plant??"
  Sometimes plain statement is best; sometimes reinforce with fresh proof, mystery, or wonderment.

**4. Has a need, doesn't connect it to your product (problem-solving ad).** Name the need and/or solution in the headline → **dramatize the need** so vividly they feel how badly they need it → present product as the inevitable solution.
  Range: bare problem ("Corns?") → problem+solution ("Stops maddening itch") → removal of limitations → problem + solution + removed limitation ("Shrinks hemorrhoids without surgery") → substitute for unpleasant task ("A ring and piston job in a tube!") → prevention ("Look, Mom! No cavities!") → emotional sharpening of one word ("Do YOU make these mistakes in English?") → when the solution has been promised too often, a verbal twist restores novelty ("How a bald-headed barber helped save my hair").

**5. Completely unaware** — not aware of the desire/need, won't admit it, it's too vague, or it's a secret that can't be said. Hardest. Also where old products end up when they fade (product name/function/desire no longer sells → product must be "reborn").
  Elimination rules: **no price, no product name (beware the logo breaking the disguise), no direct statement of function/desire/problem.** What's left = **the market itself.**
  → Write an **identification headline**: sell nothing, promise nothing; "echo an emotion, an attitude, a satisfaction" that picks people out and binds them together. Tell them what they are; define them to themselves; be the first to put a vague problem into words. Headline only sells the next paragraph; body builds logical steps from identification → awareness of the problem/desire → solution exists → your product.
  Patterns from the examples:
  - **Give words to a hidden dream** ("The University of the Night" — Lincoln studying by the fire → correspondence school)
  - **Exploit a hidden fear** ("Why Men Crack…" — coffee substitute; strong men burning out on stimulants; brand name kept out)
  - **Lead into an unacceptable problem through a universally accepted image** ("Within the Curve of a Woman's Arm" — deodorant)
  - **Project a hidden desire that can't be said in words** (Marlboro — virility through imagery only)
  - **Use a common resentment / unvoiced protest** to reach a bigger market than the direct promise ("Why haven't TV owners been told these facts" — resentment of repair contracts instead of "save $100 on repairs")
  - **Project an ultimate triumph the prospect identifies with** ("They laughed when I sat down at the piano")
  - **Project the RESULT of a problem** so people who'd reject the problem itself identify ("Often a bridesmaid but never a bride" — bad breath)
  - **Project the RESULT of an accomplishment** to attract people scared by the work ("Here's an extra $50, Grace" — reward, not study)
  Rules: far harder; can't just be a startler, joke or pretty picture ("empty headline"). **Cardinal rule: the prospect must identify with the headline before he can buy from it — his problem, his state of mind right now. It must pick out the logical prospects and reject as many people as it attracts.** Once it works, it too goes stale as the market moves. "You never step in the same river twice."
- **Style vs strategy:** styles age (verse → narrative confessions → …); when a style is new it's believed and reinforces the message; when trite, people "cannot see the message for the advertisement." Strategy doesn't age — the piano and bridesmaid ads still tap live desires and would sell if rewritten in today's idiom.

## Ch 3 — Sophistication
- Easiest of the 3 questions: a few hours of research collecting every competitor ad.
- **First in market** = zero sophistication: they believe more, get more enthusiastic, buy more readily. Happens via tech breakthrough (new product, radically better product, explosively low price) OR the ad man's insight: new application to a different market (Ovaltine: insomnia aid → body builder for skinny kids), reaching the market through an untapped medium, or discovering an unnoticed performance (Lifebuoy's medicinal smell reframed as a cure for perspiration odor + naming it "B.O."). Usually lands in a 3rd/4th-stage awareness market.

**Stage 1 (you're first):** Be simple, direct, not fancy. Name the need or the claim in the headline — nothing more. Dramatize the claim in the copy, then bring in the product and prove it works. ("NOW! LOSE UGLY FAT!")

**Stage 2 (you're second, direct claim still working):** Copy the successful claim but **enlarge it — drive it to the limit, outbid the competition.** Measure it, make it bigger. Escalation happens in steps over years (roses: 25–100 per plant → 600 mums → 17,000 blooms; cars +50hp a year → Chrysler 300). Eventually it disintegrates: claims pile up, words lose meaning ("whiter than white"), prospect confused → skeptical → claims discounted 50% → more promise poured in → regulators → sales fall.

**Stage 3 (heard every claim in every extreme):** Key factor = the market's **restorative power** (recurring instinct, unsolved tech problem, periodic replacement) — desire never fades, people quietly hope for a new way. → **A NEW MECHANISM: a new way of making the old promise work.** A different process, a fresh chance where only disappointment came before. Emphasis shifts from WHAT it does to HOW it works.
  - **Structural reversal:** in Stage 2 the headline is all claim and the mechanism is shorthand in the subhead. In Stage 3 the **mechanism takes the headline** and the claim gets shorthanded into a word ("Floats FAT right out of your body!", "First wonder drug for reducing!"). Then the lead paragraph restates the claim in full so she knows everything she's getting.

**Stage 4 (competitor's new mechanism is working):** Elaborate / enlarge the mechanism — **easier, quicker, surer; solves more of the problem; overcomes old limitations; extra benefits** ("First NO-DIET reducing wonder drug"). Same limit as Stage 2: eventually unbelievable. Then either find a NEW mechanism that is new, legitimate AND accepted as believable and significant (harder every round), or…

**Stage 5 (market exhausted, believes no ads, competitors leaving):** Same as Stage 5 awareness. Shift from promise + mechanism to **identification with the prospect himself** — bring them into the ad through identification, not desire (see Ch 8). Postum "Why Men Crack…".

**Case study — cigarettes through all 5:** 1 taste/pleasure ("They satisfy") → 2 bigger comparisons ("won't miss the sweets that make you fat") → 3 mechanisms ("It's toasted", longer length filters, "protect your T-zone") → 4 elaborated mechanisms ("harshness baked out", king-size, 9/10 doctors) → 5 pure visual identification (Marlboro virile men). Side lessons: (a) **open a new segment via identification** when you can't say it directly — "Blow some my way" opened women smokers with 4 words; (b) a crisis can open a new mass market for an existing niche product (filters after cancer scare) → and that sub-market re-runs all 5 stages (Kent trap → 20,000 filter traps → "most important ¼ inch" → dual filter → back to flavor: "tastes good like a cigarette should").
- Note: Schwartz presents copying a competitor's winning approach as an effective strategy, "not as recommendations" — objectivity, not endorsement.

## Ch 4 — Verbalization: 38 ways to strengthen a headline
Verbalization = increasing a headline's impact by HOW the claim is stated. Only after content is decided. If you're first, the bare claim is best. Otherwise bind extra images to the claim to: (1) **strengthen** it (enlarge, measure, make vivid), (2) **make it fresh again** (twist, new angle, narrative, challenge), (3) **pull them into the body** (promise info, question, partly reveal mechanism).
The 38 (my labels + tiny examples):
1. Measure the size ("20,000 filter traps") · 2. Measure the speed ("in two seconds… begins to dissolve") · 3. Compare ("six times whiter") · 4. Metaphorize ("melts away ugly fat") · 5. Sensitize — make them feel/smell/see/hear it ("the skin you love to touch") · 6. Demonstrate with a prime example (boxer can't flatten a paper cup; Rolls clock) · 7. Dramatize the claim or its result (piano; "extra $50, Grace") · 8. Paradox ("bald-headed barber saved my hair"; "beat the races by picking losers") · 9. Remove limitations ("without surgery") · 10. Associate with admired values/people (celebrity, "9 out of 10 decorators") · 11. Show how much work it does in detail ("all 5 acid-caused stomach troubles") · 12. Question ("Who else wants…?") · 13. Offer how-to information ("How to win friends…") · 14. Tie in authority ("Boss mechanic shows…") · 15. Before-and-after · 16. Stress newness ("Announcing!", "Now!") · 17. Stress exclusivity ("Only Gleem has GL-70") · 18. Challenge the reader ("Which twin has the Toni?") · 19. Case-history quotation ("Look, Mom—no cavities!") · 20. Condense — swap your product with the thing it replaces ("a ring and piston job in a tube") · 21. Symbolize — a parallel reality ("the Atlantic becomes one-fifth as long") · 22. Connect mechanism to claim ("floats fat right out") · 23. Startle by contradicting how they think the mechanism works ("hit hell out of the ball with your right hand") · 24. Connect need + claim · 25. Offer information in the ad itself ("Why men crack…", "What everybody ought to know about…") · 26. Turn claim/need into a case history ("Aunt Meg, who never married…") · 27. Name the problem/need ("daytime fatigue") · 28. Warn of pitfalls if they don't use it ("Don't invest one cent until…") · 29. Emphasize by phrasing — split, repeat ("Nobody but nobody undersells…") · 30. Show ease via a universally-overcome limitation ("If you can count to eleven…") · 31. State the difference ("the difference is right in the additives") · 32. Surprise them that old limits are gone ("crush a Hartman… Nothing!") · 33. Address people who CAN'T buy ("If you've already taken your vacation, don't read this") · 34. Address the prospect directly ("To the man who will settle for nothing less than…") · 35. Dramatize how hard it was to make ("we had to invent a whole new kind of candle") · 36. Accuse the claim of being too good ("Is it immoral to make money this easily?") · 37. Challenge their limiting beliefs ("You are twice as smart as you think") · 38. Question-and-answer.
"An infinite number of variations. Try to create your own."

## Ch 5 — Summary: creative planning (in progress)
- **3 levels of creativity:**
  1. **Word-substitute** (shallowest, most common): take a proven headline, swap in your product. Works only if same product type + same market + same time; otherwise produces **"Echo Ads"** that remind people of something else and ignore the unique product–market–timing relationship.
  2. **Formulas:** pour the headline into memorized rules. Valid only for *verbalization* (Ch 4), not for the idea itself.
  3. **Analysis** (the only real way): no answers, only guideposts and questions. Each solution works once; no creative shortcuts; must redo the effort per ad. Biggest trap: successful writers start copying themselves from their old files. "The true copy writer must argue with success."
- Copywriter must know the market better than it knows itself. **Research (motivation research)** gives: hidden/unspeakable desires, their strength and momentum, the taboos limiting how they can be said, splits in the market, early reactions to your phrasing. But **a research finding is never a headline** — it's a direction: first where NOT to go, then the general area of the answer. Turning it into an idea still takes full creativity.
- The campaign is a **conversation with the market**: you feed ideas, it feeds back reactions — mostly negative (failures, limits), and only the numbers, not the causes. Ask *why* → those "why" questions become research questions → new theme/headline/campaign. (Today: comments, refunds, reviews, ad metrics.)
- **Product personality:** a product/brand has a whole personality to the buyer (Cadillac = quality, prestige, performance…). **One trait always summarizes it best** (Cadillac = quality). Feature that trait in headlines, blend others into it, then in the body expand into all appeals — "an ever-enlarging pyramid of persuasion" ending in the product.
- **The only prevention headline that sells:** people won't picture future disaster happening to *themselves*, but readily picture it happening to **loved ones** (kids' cavities, family without life insurance). Sell prevention through others.
- **Splinter markets:** a market isn't one piece (weight loss = appearance group vs health group → same appeal, different mechanisms: speed/ease vs safety/permanence). A small budget can own a splinter defined by *sociology, not geography* — changes everything: headline, identification copy, mechanism, proof, media, distribution. Win it, then use that win as the point of difference to invade the main market ("relied on twice as often by women who HAD to lose weight").
- **Summary of Part I process (days–months):** 1) analyze the market — breadth, depth, the emotional forces creating it, focus them into one image/desire/need; 2) study the product — physical vs functional, all performances, focused to one image/identification/claim with the most emotional force → = **the theme** (desire + satisfaction / need + solution / identification + expression); 3) measure awareness + sophistication + how much they care → **point of entry** for the headline (product, price, performance, satisfaction, need, or only the market itself). → Then you write 5–10 words. **"These five or ten words will make up about 90% of the value of your ad."** All-or-nothing.
- You don't "get" an idea — you build/unfold it: dig it out of research, wring it out of the product, read, listen, experiment. **Cardinal rule: find the ONE element that makes this product + market unique.** Unique product–market–timing → unique solution. Part I gives "not molds, but compasses."
- Two classes of guideposts: analytical (break into parts, find the most important, start there) → building (remove limiting factors, add everything that strengthens). = conception → execution. Body copy's job (Part II): **build conviction** — desire PLUS belief that the product will DO it.

# PART II — Body copy

## Ch 6 — Inside the prospect's mind: 3 dimensions
- Headline flags him down; **body copy does the selling by altering his vision of reality** — creates a new world where your product is the fulfillment of the desire that made him respond.
- **Length of the ad depends on 3 factors** (= 3 dimensions of his mind):
  1. How much copy to build **desire** to its greatest strength.
  2. How much more to make him feel **comfortable and complimented** by the product — fit it into the life he's building.
  3. How much more to make him **believe** it — compensate for existing prejudices/beliefs.
  These also set structure, development, style and pace.
- **1. Desires** — physical (thin, strong, no acne), material (money, car, dress), sensual (cold beer, soft bed). Can't create, diminish or fight them; **can expand, sharpen, channel, give them a goal.** First (sometimes only) task: make him want — picture every moment of fulfillment until he practically lives in it, then offer the product. → *Intensification* (Ch 7).
- **2. Identifications** — the roles he wants to play, personality traits he wants the product to build/project, status. Non-material; they make every purchase do double duty (low-cal food → thin AND radiant, youthful personality; car → power AND visible success). Never openly spoken. Job: **put them behind the product — make him feel the select group he joins; picture the people who live in your product's world.** → *Identification* (Ch 8).
- **3. Beliefs** — opinions, attitudes, prejudices, fragments of knowledge, his "emotionalized reason," how he accepts/rejects facts. **Advertising doesn't argue with them and can't change them** (it's not education; no time). Accept reality, then alter it by exploiting its tendencies. **Believing = fitting new facts into established patterns.** Beliefs are a filter your info must pass; his reasoning habits are channels your copy must follow "or die." Use **HIS kind of logic, not yours**, to prove: it satisfies his desire, it works, his kind of people rely on it, nothing else does it as well. → *Gradualization + Redefinition* (Ch 9–10).
- In the real ad you weave all three into one fluid path → *Blending & Reinforcement* (Ch 14).

## Ch 7 — Intensification: strengthening desire (in progress)
- Selling = expanding desire **horizontally** (more people) and **vertically** (sharpen/magnify it until it beats skepticism, lethargy and price).
- "Advertising is the literature of desire… our twentieth-century Wish Book." Desires in the prospect's head are **blurs** — vague, unworded, at a fraction of their power. **Job: fill vague desire with concrete images, show every way it can be fulfilled, multiply its strength by the number of satisfactions you suggest.**
- Copywriter = "script writer for your prospect's dreams… chronicler of his future." Show in minute detail all the tomorrows the product makes possible — everything he gets, everything he leaves behind, everything he might miss.
- **The sharper and more numerous the pictures, the more he demands the product and the less important the price.**
- How much space: (1) medium — short media (catalog, small space, radio/TV) need **compression** (boil images into a few key words) + **campaign** (repeat those key words with progressive embellishment across many ads); long media (big space, direct mail) → (2) how many *fresh* ways you can show it without repetition/boredom.
- **He takes away only ONE dominant image** — but each new angle on it makes it sharper and heavier. Walk around the product in your imagination; show every way it enters his life.
- Two enemies: (a) phrasing he's seen in competitors' ads (sophistication applies to body copy too), (b) repeating your own phrasing → **"You cannot repeat, but you can reinforce."** Each fresh setting of the same promise reinforces the ones before.
- Examples are from mail order because it must make the whole sale in one ad.
**The techniques:**
1. **First presentation — blunt, thorough, completely detailed description** of the product's appearance or the results it gives. Pile on specific numbers and sensory detail (rose ad: blooms per month → 4,076 roses; cluster size vs your outstretched hands; "enough roses to fill every room"; ends with "showplace of the neighborhood" = identification). Slide projector: the room gasps, scene by scene through the kids' eyes. Model jet: their faces next week, exact dimensions/colors.
2. **Put the product in action** — not just how it looks and what it gives, but exactly HOW it does it, step by step in motion (jet on its control line: lifts, streaks, stalls, dives, lands; lure: fuel, cast, shudder, buzz, descend, climb, "even when you're asleep"). Often ends with a price comparison.
3. **Bring the reader in** — put HIM in the middle of the product-in-action story; a verbal demonstration of what happens to him **the first day he owns it** ("Picture this… next weekend you go down to your car…"). Power-booster ad: a stoplight race with a same-year car; he sees the other driver's startled face in his mirror.
4. **Show him how to test your claims** — turn the demonstration into a test he runs himself, in the most specific, dramatic way, with immediate results. Spark plug ad: note how fast the car creeps at idle → install → it now creeps 4–6 mph faster; tach RPMs up 200. Memory book: turn to page 39, read 8 pages, write 20 facts, next morning amaze everyone. Exact steps + exact numbers = proof he can picture.
5. **Stretch the benefits out in time** — show the product working over weeks and months, a continuous flow of benefits in stages ("But this is just the beginning!" → second stage → "the most remarkable part of all" → third stage → neighbors touching the flowers to see if they're real).
6. **Bring in an audience** — other people's eyes give a fresh perspective: celebrities (testimonial), average people the reader identifies with (narration like the piano ad, or **case histories** — "men and women… reported… lost 17, 25, even 34 pounds").
7. **Show experts approving** — "There is nothing so astounding as the astonishment of experts." Surprise + competition + discovery (college horticulturists' test field of champion roses → the new rose outblooms them 15x; expert: "they'd never believe it").
8. **Compare, contrast, prove superiority** — lay the old product's disadvantages beside the new one's advantages, in parallel ("While your friends were paying $3.95… members were getting the same book for 99c" ×3). "Instead of X… you get Y."
9. **Picture the black side too (Heaven-or-Hell)** — paint the problem you're freeing them from in full black color: "You irritate the wound, and then you apply the salve." Gives two currents: **repulsion from the old + attraction to the new.** Formats: narration/testimonial, "pitch" ridicule of the old product, or straight parallel description item by item. Spark plug: pull out your old plug → FILTH, corroded, pitted firing point robbing 20 horsepower → "At last! A plug so smart that it thinks!" → "But that's just the beginning… still just the beginning."
10. **Show how easy it is to get the benefits** — every point where the product touches his life (price, availability, ease of use, durability, portability, maintenance, even unwrapping the box) is a fresh angle. "30 seconds work" repeated → 8 improvements (contrast tiny effort vs huge benefit).
11. **Use metaphor, analogy, imagination** — go beyond raw fact (Cody course: a "100% Self-Correcting Device" = Mr. Cody standing at your elbow whispering the correction; Big Ben "first he whispers, then he shouts").
12. **Summarize before you're done** — rule for how many angles to use: **use another fresh perspective only while it keeps building the dominant desire; if it isn't different/dramatic enough to renew interest, cut it.** Test: re-read the ad days later to find where reinforcement became repetition. Two summary devices:
    - **The "catalog"** — condensed list of every performance/benefit/application, one line each, no elaboration. Type A expands desire **horizontally** (more applications/prospects — blowtorch "101 jobs": laying tile, thawing pipes…). Type B expands **vertically** (piles desire on desire — fish lure "frees you forever from…", "It means that tomorrow…", "their eyes will almost pop out").
    - Logic: the body so far was a **rifle** (one dominant desire, maximum intensity, demands accuracy). The catalog is the last-minute **shotgun** hedge — effect of sheer magnitude/infinite possibilities so any one may close the sale.
13. **Put the guarantee to work** — make the guarantee the climax: a last summary of the performances, each re-asserted with "or your money back" (spark plugs: "must give you up to 9 more mpg… or full price back; must give up to 31 more hp…"; plant pellets: "Use them to create super-flowers… super-vegetables… if your garden isn't the showplace of your neighborhood in one month — return the empty package"). Also reframe price per use ("only a few pennies a plant").
- "At the end of your ad as well as at its beginning—use Intensification."

**Intensification across a campaign (national advertising / repeated exposure — = running ads to the same audience):**
- Mail order = one ad says everything, then is "frozen" (even typography) and run until it exhausts. National = many insertions → novelty dies fast → problem becomes the theme for a **series**, building desire across the series.
- Balance: keep the dominant image sharp/identifiable enough to cash in past desire, yet vary it enough that they read again.
- Two jobs: (1) **compress the dominant image into one statement or picture** that sells the first time and keeps selling on repeat; (2) **create a series of fresh variations/perspectives** on it. = **Central Campaign Idea + continuous restatement in fresh disguises.** The central idea usually can't stay the headline every time.
- Patterns: **Fixed message + varied opening** (Colgate Gardol "invisible shield": a different startling 10-second demo each time, then ritual identical wording) · **"Rule and Example"** — exact same headline, constantly changing pictures that renew it ("Which twin has the Toni?", "Does she or doesn't she?") · **Underlying focal point only** — no repeated slogan; one appeal maintained in every element: photos, type, style, props (Cadillac = quality) · **The product itself as the focal point** — each ad reveals one new fact about it, nothing repeated (Volkswagen: severe simplicity, product close-ups…).
  → Modern translation: one angle/concept, many hooks/creatives; or same hook line over many visuals.
- VW "Honest Car" series: each ad = one building block (Think Small, Lemon, Our new model, open the window before you close the door, Our number one salesman). Each headline (a) touches the dominant desire (more value for car money), (b) expresses the campaign theme, (c) pulls into the body — mostly via an **apparent contradiction** with the reader's belief or with the photo above it, or by promising info explaining a provocative request.
- **"The Creative Gamble":** product, trademark and performance left OUT of the headline, betting the headline can drag them into the body where the real selling happens. Fail = wasted page; win = 2–3x the ad.

## Ch 8 — Identification: build a saleable personality into the product
- Desire (Ch 7) is obvious and open. Identification is a second, subtler, partly unconscious desire — **not for satisfaction but for expression**. Its rise is "the great revolution in merchandising of our time." Used as an adjunct to desire-building.
- Schwartz invented the names of his techniques; stress is on **active** processes the writer performs (Identification = the writer building the realization of the buyer's identity-longing into the product).
- **Identification = the prospect's desire to act out roles, define himself to the world, express the qualities he values and the positions he's reached.** Use it two ways: (1) **product as the instrument for achieving the role**, (2) **product as the acknowledgement that the role is already achieved.**
- **Every product should give two reasons to buy:** (1) fulfills a physical want/need (the satisfaction), (2) a *particular way* of fulfilling it that defines him to others as a certain kind of person (the role) = the "super-functional" value, built by merchandising, not engineering. (Food is bought as modern, slimming, cosmopolitan…) Each coveted role = one more mass desire to harness.
- **Two kinds of roles:**
  1. **Character roles** — adjectives ("progressive", "chic", "charming", "well-read"). Mastered, then they must be acknowledged/admired. Product helps 3 ways: helps master the role (philosophy book), speeds/simplifies mastery (speed-reading course), **symbolizes mastery to win admiration (the fine bookshelf)** — the most important. "At least half of all purchases made today cannot be understood in terms of function alone" ($5,000 150-mph car driven on 35-mph parkways = "sportsman").
     - Character roles live in the subconscious, are never openly claimed, can't be tested → **the prospect believes the character role you assign him far more readily than performance claims or achievement roles.** Flattering + expressed through a non-verbal symbol (test pilot smoking) → no claim to reject, no test to pass, "consolation-without-cost." **Use it to supplement the verbal claims in every ad.**
  2. **Achievement roles** — nouns/titles created by society (Executive, Home Owner, Man-on-his-way-up, Good Mother, Fashion Setter, Career Woman…). Invisible, so they must be **displayed** → translated into products. "We are known… by the products we own." The bride: ring, trousseau, new everything = a "material personality." Promotions → trade up (Ford → Buick → Cadillac).
- **When your product does the same job as competitors and price isn't a factor, the choice depends almost entirely on the role it offers** — create that role in the ad.
- Process = same as desire: **discover** which roles the market will accept for this product, which it rejects, which is most compelling → **present the chosen role so vividly it becomes irresistible.**
- **Limits:** you can't force an unrealistic identification. People already assign characteristics to products (from structure, history, cost, role in their lives or others' lives). True or false, they're "as hard as rock." Contradict them → prison walls. **Build on them** → they become your strongest selling points.
- Products with built-in prestige (sports car, diamond) can lend it to others. Most products have none → **you create prestige by building on characteristics they already have, using them as a bridge** from current image → desired prestige image.
- Most-wanted American roles (1960s, mass market): virile/feminine · exciting, unique, fun, adventurous · friendly, well-liked · important, influential, correct · modern, up-to-date · above all, **successful**. Splinter markets may differ.
- **Primary image:** the product already has a personality when you get it (cigarette = virile; piston ring = precision; appliance = time-saving). Two steps:
  1. **Change the intensity of the primary image** — dramatize it if favorable; tone it down if negative/neutral. Marlboro intensified virility 3 ways (virile men + virile occupations + the tattoo — the "Creative Gamble") = repetition through variation.
  2. **Use it as a logical link to bring in more favorable images.** You can't discard a negative image and substitute a new one — "people just won't believe that a product is what it isn't." Instead, **incorporate it into a larger image, lower its intensity, use it as an accepted bridge** to more compelling appeals. ("Blow some my way": the man is kept — the accepted image — but darkened, subdued, leading the eye to the woman; romance, escape, daring layered in → she accepts the whole scene → accepts the line.)
  - Only limit on how many images you add: **they must emerge from the primary image and be logically consistent with its broadest definition.** Then weave in success (tuxedo), adventure (boat/plane/horse), romance (a girl), mystery (dark background/briefcase), culture (rare book). Multi-image symbols (a fine painting = success + culture + intellect) both **widen the market** (pull in fringe people) and **deepen** the attraction of the core.
  - Worked example — **neutral product (piston rings)**: inventory primary images (mechanical, precise, unseen) + primary situation (replacement by a mechanic when there's trouble). Keep the limits, then glamorize each element to the brink: Mercedes SL-300 instead of a sedan; spotless sports-car shop; mature, precise mechanic; young, wiry, virile owner who races for sport (roll bar), loves the car (shine), precise (chronometer), casual about success (slacks); expert advising expert; rings installed not because of trouble but as **high-performance equipment like a supercharger.** Everything (angle, light, composition) builds the emotion of precision. Reader won't notice each detail but senses the world and wants in.
  - Said in words, these images feel "corny"; **shown visually they're implied, slip in unnoticed and are accepted.** Verbal identification needs different methods (Ch 14).
- **Warning — believability of images:** campaigns die when the market can't identify with the image (elite society image to sell a mass-market deodorant soap → disbelief spread to the performance claims too). Two checks:
  1. Do people already believe the product has these traits? Can they identify themselves — their present life or **their next step up** — with them? Both yes → use it.
  2. If not → find a **"believability-bridge"**: start from what they already believe and build up. If they believe the trait but don't see it in their life → insert a first image they identify with instantly and hang the target image on it (Marlboro virility made yacht/opera-hat images acceptable).
  - "If you demand that your prospect jump across a believability-chasm, your ad will fail. If… you build a bridge… starting on his side—then he will let you lead him almost anywhere."
- **Identification from the physical product itself** — 3 areas: (1) appearance, (2) components/structure, (3) technical background. Each can hold existing primary images or have related images grafted on.
  - Appearance: borrow dramatic images from the extreme forms of your product category (racing fins, sports-car hubcaps → family sedan), then weave in images unrelated to function.
  - Chemical products (liquids, powders, sprays) have arbitrary shapes → borrow images from the product's background, components, or society's values. **Micrin mouthwash: put it in the exact shape of medical glassware + ice-blue liquid → "This is doctor's liquid. This is medicine. This works."** The container became the best ad illustration. **Rule: if the product's background holds excitement, drama, quality or believability, express it in the product, the package, or the ad.**
  - Components: same idea — electronic parts → show the radar, missiles, jets that use the same parts. Ordinary life lacks adventure; letting the buyer take part in "frontier explorations" welds a strong appeal onto the product.

## Ch 9 — Gradualization: make them believe the claims BEFORE you state them
- Recap: Desire (path to the goal goes through the product; visualize every drop of satisfaction) + Identification (crystallize self-definitions, embody them in the product = symbolic extension of personality). **Neither is enough alone** — both must fuse with **Belief** → **Absolute Conviction** (certainty, feeling right in the choice, assured of the promise) = the ultimate goal.
- **What belief is:** his mental picture of the world (facts, how it works, values) + — more important — the **emotional security** those beliefs give him (a world that makes sense and won't blow up tomorrow). The need for secure beliefs is as strong as any physical desire.
- Beliefs were laid down in childhood; shattering one = asking him to become a child again. Unless "frightened beyond belief," he won't.
- **Basic rule of belief:** violate an established belief in the slightest degree (content or direction) → nothing you promise can save the ad. BUT channel his belief behind even ONE small claim → that fully-believed claim outsells all competitors' half-questioned promises (can even support otherwise-absurd claims). Paddle downstream.
- For advertising, **belief is immutable** — every statement must fit his version of "the facts" right now. The ad's job isn't to change them but to **extend** them: a **bridge of belief** from today's facts to the facts he must believe to accept your claim.
- **Gradualization = start with facts he already accepts, lead him logically and comfortably through a gradual succession of more remote facts, each prepared for in turn.** Not the same as proof/reason-why/testimonials (those are Mechanization Ch 11, Concentration Ch 12, Verification Ch 14). Gradualization governs the ad's **structure / architecture** — the arrangement of claims, images and proofs so desire AND conviction strengthen step by step.
  - Content of the ad ← dominant desire. Illustrations ← identification. **Development/sequence ← the facts he believes and how he passes acceptance from one to the next.**
- **The architecture of belief:** every claim has 2 sources of strength: (1) its content, (2) **the preparation made for it** (already in his mind, or laid down earlier in the ad). So you can strengthen a claim by (1) more intense content, or (2) **moving it** — placing it after material that prepares it, so it's accepted instantly without question.
- "Effective advertising… is built—not of words—but of reactions." Words → an architecture of reactions → a **stream of acceptances** with sequence, direction and a goal (absolute conviction).
- **New definition of awareness: "readiness to accept."** Headline must (a) awaken interest/desire AND (b) be **accepted as true at first glance.** Interest + believability determine the headline. Especially at Stage 5, effectiveness depends as much on willingness to believe as on the promise.
- **Paradox: your most powerful claim is not always your most powerful headline.** Without supporting evidence already in his mind, he thinks it's exaggerated, false, or not about him. Mail-order proof: when a power-claim head fails, split-test a head with **fewer claims but more believable**, then build a belief-bridge from it to the exact same claims, now prepared step by step.
- **Worked example — TV repair manual (1951):** Prospects had to believe (1) they can save money doing their own repairs, (2) they're capable of it. Both true, but they saw themselves as non-handymen and TVs as monsters. "Save up to $100 a year on your TV repairs!" was tested and lost money.
  1. Headline **crystallizes a universal resentment** (repair bills, $60 service contracts, a few crooked shops): "Why haven't TV owners been told these facts?" — no claim, only disclosure + implied foul play = something he agrees with. **Acceptance #1.**
  2. **Inclusion question** that seems to limit the market but ~95% answer yes: "Was your set purchased after the spring of 1947?" **Acceptance #2** → a **"Habit of Agreement."**
  3. Only now the first promise: "Then here is the full, uncensored story of how you can avoid those $15–$20 repair bills — avoid those $30–$60 a year service fees — and still get perfect pictures." Belief is loaned to it by:
     - grammar: "Was your…" → "Then…" implies **exclusion** (only works in certain cases) → adds credence (drop "Then" and it loses half its force);
     - **describing universal symptoms** (bills, fees) inside the promise → more "yes, I have those" carried into the savings claim (vs bare "save $15… save $90");
     - **the cure is left deliberately vague** — no mention that he'll do repairs himself yet, because he's not prepared; he'd turn the page.
  4. Symptom questions: "How many times this week have you had to get up to fix a jumpy picture? … ghosts?" → yes, yes, yes; each yes proves the ad is about him. 7–8 agreements → next promise: "90% of these breakdowns are unnecessary!" + same sets play perfectly in manufacturers' test rooms.
  5. **Goal conclusion 1 (sets are dependable)** proven before any mention of him touching the set: vivid test details (24/7 for up to 17 months, 120 miles from the station, a warehouse of appliances, steel-ribbed buildings) → then expert authority + logic built on the accepted condition: "If your set were properly cared for, as these sets were…" → it breaks down only once a year → you save the fees. **Returns to the 3rd-sentence claims almost word for word and now proves them** — proof woven into promise; chain of proof on proof. (= Intensification: same theme 7 ways.)
  6. **Goal conclusion 2 (you can fix minor problems)** slipped in disguised as a continuation: "**And, most important, these experts have discovered** that you do not have to be a handyman…" Acceptance carried over by: (a) **paragraph parallelism** (same form as the accepted paragraphs, no new subhead), (b) lead word "And", (c) "most important" tie-in, (d) echoing "these experts have discovered."
  7. Then: TV is like your body — gives warning signals; minor outside-control adjustments prevent breakdowns; without the knowledge → bad picture → repairman.
  8. **Pay-off conclusion, with the force of a syllogism:** "You pay a repairman—not for his work—but for his knowledge. If you had that knowledge yourself—then you would not have to pay him at all." → then the book.
- **Rules:**
  - Gradualization = stating a claim so it gets the **greatest possible acceptance**.
  - **"Belief ultimately depends upon structure."** Desire depends on promise; belief in the promise depends on how much preparation it got before he's asked to accept it.
  - **"One fully-believed promise has ten times the sales power of ten partially-believed promises."** Piling promises → more sales resistance + regulator trouble. Invest instead in the believability structure of the original justifiable promise.
- **Belief-building devices:**
  1. **The Inclusion Question** — he sees you're talking about him; once he's in the yes-group your recommendations mean more ("Ask yourself these nine questions: 1. Do you find it increasingly difficult to cope…").
  2. **Detailed Identification** — instead of questions, detail the symptoms/problems that are his reasons for wanting it → "you've been there yourself" ("I don't care whether your child is six or twenty… how poor his memory… how terrified of mathematics…"). **Must be accurate — research first**, or the believability net shatters.
  3. **Contradiction of present (false) beliefs** — "I know you think this is true; I'll show you it's false." Best with strong authority. Goal is **loosening old beliefs** (old limitations are passing), then introduce the positive claim right after ("forget everything you've heard about how 'old' you must look at thirty… Because—starting with this moment—you are about to enter a new world…").
  4. **The Language of Logic** — interlace every new promise with words that signal it follows from what's been proved: centuries of courts, science and detective stories mean **the words themselves carry conviction**, regardless of content. "The reason for this is clear…", "There is a basic, underlying reason…", "Here's why…", "Therefore…", "This has been proved by thousands…", "Sound impossible? Not at all. It's actually as simple…", "And, most important of all, is the fact that…", "They discovered—in case after case—that…". Woven in so subtly he never notices; as emotional as the language of desire.
  5. **Syllogistic Thinking** — the mechanics of logic: prove the product works by a chain where each statement leads inevitably to the next (spark plug: gas fires by spark → explosion pushes piston → **the larger the spark, the more powerful the explosion → the more power from your gas**; poor explosion = waste, good = more mpg). Constant equation: spark → power → performance; size = power; his plug has the biggest spark. Result: a **feeling of inevitability** — not just told it works, shown proof it must. This structure is a real, traceable thing under the words; find it in ads, codify it, reuse it.
  6. **Other belief forms** (form/structure itself creates believability):
     - **Contingency structures** — "If… then…", "Is your… then…"
     - **Repetition of proof / echoing** — "These experts found… These experts found…"
     - **Promise → Belief → Promise variation** — follow every promise sentence with proof/verification/documentation so he never gets breathing space to question.
     - **Paragraph parallelism** — reuse the exact word structure of an accepted statement to borrow its acceptance for a new claim.
     - Some are words, some are reasoning chains, some are just physical layout on the page. Goal of all: continued acceptance, prevent rejection, build conviction.

## Ch 10 — Redefinition: remove objections to the product
- Every product has resistance (at minimum: he has to pay). Some have built-in drawbacks that repel and will kill the sale unless redefined.
- **3 kinds of drawbacks:** (1) too complicated / hard to use, (2) not important enough — appeal not broad enough, (3) costs too much vs its class.
- **Redefinition = giving the product a new definition: "it's THIS rather than THAT."** Goal: remove the roadblock — ideally before he even knows it's there.
- **Concept-judo / flip-flop (simplest, often best):** turn the liability into the asset with one idea. Lifebuoy's horrible medicinal smell → focus on HIS body odor ("B.O."), which drives people away → ordinary soap isn't strong enough → you need super-powerful soap → **proof: you can smell its power the moment you open the wrapper.** Use whenever possible.
**1. Simplification (product sounds too hard):**
  - TV manual: the failed ad led with "Do your own TV repairs" — confronted him with the hard thing before making it easy ("it's easy" in the next paragraph didn't save it).
  - The winning ad talked about HIS world (breakdowns, expenses) and a better world (expert care = trouble-free) and then joined them through the product = **"the turn"** (pitchman term) — a delicate, jar-free transition.
  - **State his fear openly but framed as a promise** ("you do not have to be a handyman"). Hold back the scary word ("repair") until he's ready — use a softer one ("coax").
  - Then **redefine the scary word**: TV = like your body (familiar, explains away mystery); problems = "warning signals"/"symptoms" not "breakdowns"/"repairs"; fixes = "a few minor adjustments on the outside controls"; real breakdowns isolated to the rare 5%. → no reason left not to do it himself.
  - **For a genuinely easier new invention: shouting "EASY" doesn't work.** Two answers: (1) redefine, (2) mechanize the new simplicity (Ch 11). "Innovation without acceptance is valueless." The more people know a thing is difficult and the more revolutionary your product, the more resistance → **redefine the entire field before you bring in the product.**
  - Cody English ad (memorize it, don't just study it): English made easy with a few facts — 69 words make up over half of what we say/write; only a dozen principles of punctuation; 25 typical errors = 9/10 of mistakes → "no more than fifteen minutes a day." = **shrink the task into a small, finite, countable list.**
**2. Escalation (works, easy, but not important enough for a mass market):**
  - **Broaden the horizon of benefits / redefine the role the product plays in his life / show it pays off in dozens of situations a day, where he least expects it.**
  - (a) **Shift the benefit** to a more desired area: 40 years later, English no longer sold on avoiding mistakes → redefined as a **means to a bigger end** (persuasion, winning people) → feature the part with most value for that (vocabulary): "Word Power Machine… hold others spellbound… make anybody like you."
  - (b) **Increase the product's importance** by showing something he badly wants hinges on it: "You pay $2,000–$4,000 for your car. And a single 99c part robs you of the real power…"; speed math = "an absolute necessity for your future."
  - (c) **Show it's needed almost every waking minute**, not once a week: "Your entire life is spent trying to get others to do what you want—without friction." He must agree → importance redefined.
**3. Price Reduction (costs too much):**
  - It's too expensive only because he's comparing it to its own category. **Switch the comparison to a more expensive standard.** Never say "costs a little more but worth it."
  - Spark plugs at $1.49 (1.5x standard, 2x discount price) — 10 bargain signals in two paragraphs: "practically made by hand" → "would have to sell for as high as $5 each" → whole set "30 or 40 dollars" (he winces) → "get the price down so low" (now legitimate) → "pay for themselves 12 times in one year" (reward + another bigger comparison) → "astonishing proposition" → a **condition** ("if you will check your car's performance… and tell your friends") → "prepared to do" (a favor) → "**You can have**" (not "you can buy") → "only $1.49 each."
- Gradualization works by **structure** (arrangement of facts/phrases); Redefinition by **rearranging perspective.** Both operate below conscious awareness.

## Ch 11 — Mechanization: verbally prove the product does what you claim
- Copy exists in two places: words on the page AND the **planned series of reactions in his mind.** Reading = a **silent dialogue**: you feed ideas/images/emotions; he feeds back reactions — including **inevitable demands/questions you must answer or the copy fails.**
- **3 classes of demands:**
  1. **"Tell me more."** — more information, image, desire.
  2. **"Oh yeah? Who says so?"** — proof.
  3. **"How does it work?"** — mechanism.
- Write as copywriter AND prospect simultaneously; sense the **exact point** each demand arises and switch direction to supply it there. This is where many good ads break down — you'll rework the same "unimportant" paragraph for hours. (Covered more in Ch 14.)
- **Mechanism = demonstrate in words, logically, exactly HOW it delivers the result** = "Reason Why" copy (Hopkins its master). Question is never "should I?" but **"how much?"** — depends on awareness of the mechanism:
  - **Stage 1 — Name it.** He already knows and accepts the mechanism (competitors paid to teach it) → just name it in bold, then beat them on price/features ("Electronic Light Setter… Push-Button Zomar Lens… Only $135"). Catalog/retail copy.
  - **Stage 2 — Describe it.** He doesn't understand the mechanism (or everyone shares it and you need a new edge). Classic **Promise → Reason Why**: strong quick promise, intensify it until it starts to sound "too good to be true" and she's asking "How?" → **then switch direction and sell the mechanism** (Rinso: suds float the dirt off). **Rule: mechanism copy is not scientific discourse — never dull or merely factual; load it with promise and emotion. It sells a secondary claim that proves the primary claim.** Sophisticated markets need far more mechanism, deeper explanation, maybe a "miracle ingredient."
  - **Stage 3 — Feature it.** Sophisticated market, promises sound alike, price war is suicide → mechanism goes **into the headline** and becomes the main claim ("Floats fat right out of your body", "Shrinks hemorrhoids without surgery"). = A NEW WAY, a new chance, even if everything else failed.
  - Summary rule: they assume they know how it works / claim is so new they don't care → a word or phrase. Not sure → describe it in selling language until they have enough reason-why. **Exceptionally strong/dramatic mechanism or you want to prove superiority → "sell hell out of that mechanism."**
- **Price cuts need a mechanism too.** People don't believe the truth or a bare price cut ("no increase in sales"). **Every bargain needs a reason why.** Robert Collier: a mill overstocked because of unseasonable weather → offered surplus Madras below cost → "Before the price goes up!" = a mechanism within a mechanism (weather → overstock → markdown), plus quality as a counter-desire throughout. Starts with the mechanism, bargain claims only 6 paragraphs later (Creative Gamble) → 10x the believability for every bargain word after.

## Ch 12 — Concentration: destroy the alternatives
- No copy sells a product; it sells **a way of satisfying a desire.** Commercial desire → competition is universal.
- **5 ways to beat competition:** (1) **superiority of product** — the ultimate weapon; most great claims "come from the assembly line." If your copy is better than your product, send it to the client and tell him to make it real. Even the best product needs copy that gets the first purchase before repeat sales can pay back. (2) **superiority of promise** — stronger (more desire), wider (more buyers), more believable (wins skeptics too). (3) **product-role** (identification/prestige). (4) **response and reaction** — one-up: escalate claims, shift mechanisms, invade new markets. (5) **direct attack = Concentration.**
- 1–4 **ignore the competition** (act as if no other way exists). Best when you dominate the field, are protecting loyal customers, or your story is so strong/fresh nobody matches it — then don't give the rival the prestige of being attacked.
- **Use direct attack when your budget is much smaller and most prospects are already his customers** — you must crack his image and break their loyalty before you can redirect their desire.
- **Rule: never attack a weakness unless you provide the solution to that weakness at the same time.** Biased attack for your own good → skepticism and dislike. Attack shown to be **for HIS good** (your product removes this weakness) → he'll question even ingrained loyalty.
- **Concentration = point out the competition's weaknesses → emphasize how they disserve him → prove your product gives him what he wants without them.** Uses everything: Intensification (penalties of staying with the old), Gradualization (logical cause of the weakness and how it's now cured), Mechanization (prove yours removes it).
- **Structure A — point-by-point "Bad / Good / Bad / Good"** (spark plug vs fire injector, "Mechanics and engineers read this carefully"): each plug weakness immediately answered by the injector's strength; **parallel sentence structure** sharpens contrast; **word choice contrast** (weak "spark" vs strong "fire"; "thin skimpy spark" vs "heavy powerful flame"); **definition/redefinition** ("most wasteful way" vs "most efficient way"); redefinition by analogy (injector "breaks in" and gets better with use); **every benefit has its documentary mechanism** ("because the electrode is burning away"). Closes by piling on authority (U.S. Air Force pays premium prices for them).
- **Structure B — time sequence** (for a familiar recurring bad experience): "What happens to you now with what you use?" → "What will happen when you switch?" Reducing pill: (1) **define/redefine** the old way (ordinary plans are "passive", depend on your willpower, only dull hunger, can't *actively* burn fat), (2) **cause-and-effect logic** ("So what happened?"), (3) condense her **own lived experience** step by step (willpower, torture, lost 5–12 lbs, willpower snapped, fat came back heavier) → she nods at every line → stream of acceptances, (4) the climax line tied in with "And" **implies the pills caused the diet's failure** inside a sentence she can't disagree with, (5) hero product was foreshadowed by the accusation (they can't "actively burn" fat) → "And so you tried another… and another" → doctors had the answer → mechanism + authority (Nobel winner's discovery) → case histories → back into Intensification.
- Can be boiled down to a phrase: "Shrinks hemorrhoids WITHOUT surgery" = implied weakness of alternatives + your compensating promise. **Judge any mechanism by the reaction it produces, not its length:** did he question a habit, shift a loyalty, take a chance on you?

## Ch 13 — Camouflage: borrow conviction
- People buy a publication/medium to learn what's happening, be entertained or kept current — **not for its ads.** They **trust** it, and some of that trust spills onto its ads ("they wouldn't run it if it weren't true"). Lose faith in the medium → its ads stop working. **Believability of the medium matters more than circulation.**
- Over time the reader is conditioned to receive truth in that medium's **style, format and phrasing** → the phrasing alone carries "an aura of truth." That's **stored believability — a believability reflex** you tap by matching it.
- **3 ways to borrow it:**
  1. **Format** — approximate the medium's look as closely as possible (ideally let them typeset it; use their headline-to-body transition, illustrations, subheads, column breaks) so the reader shifts gears as little as possible from "editorial" to "ad" and belief carries through every line. **"A single change in format can add 50% to your readership, and your results."** WSJ example: same copy as a mildly successful generic magazine ad, adapted to Journal type, old-fashioned double subheads, rules, left-aligned subheads, an ugly **line drawing** of the author (because the Journal uses drawings, not photos) → ran 19 times monthly with no drop-off; ~2x believability, pull and staying power. Worth the extra cost; do it for every important medium.
  2. **Phraseology** — use the stock phrases that carry belief in that medium. Newspapers: dateline, city of origin, byline ("SKIN SPECIALIST DEMONSTRATES HOW TO RINSE AWAY YOUR BLACKHEADS — By [name] — New York, N.Y.—A leading doctor today showed…") → reads as a report, not a sales pitch. Direct mail: imitate the envelopes people open instantly (refund checks, government letters, dividend notices, confidential reports, pricey newsletters). Radio/TV: news format and sound — clipped newscaster phrasing, insider analyst tone, documentary hand-held feel. "Study the channels of communication that people believe in. Adopt their tone… Camouflage them."
  3. **Mood** — "advertising language" triggers automatic skepticism by its very look. Two moods escape the hard-sell stereotype:
     - **Understatement** — simplicity, few color words, fewer adjectives than expected, short sentences whose tone *falls* at the end, kid yourself a little, make the point quietly then stop (VW air-cooled engine ad: no radiator. Or hoses. Or water pump. Or rust.). Also study Hopkins/Kennedy for sincerity without whimsy. Rare → extra effective, especially in continuous campaigns.
     - **Deadly Sincerity** — lean over backwards to point out the offer's flaws so the benefits are believed more deeply. Ordinary ads never knock their product → big impact in crowded fields. "To the man who will settle for nothing less than the presidency of his firm… willing to make the incredible sacrifices… This is a private advertisement. It is not meant for ninety-nine men out of every hundred…" → non-advertising phrasing, unpredictability, nearly the believability of a factual report.

## Ch 14 — The Final Touches: weaving it all together (in progress)
- Review: Part I = find the headline. Part II = lead him from the headline's interest into mounting conviction through 3 interlocking paths: **Desire (intensification) · Identification (product role) · Believability (structure).** Last job: weave every promise, image, device and structure into one unit that holds attention start to finish.
**Verification — placing authorities and proof:**
- Proof = statistics, tests, testimonials, authorities, trends, documentation, seals, awards — any fact showing it does what you say.
- **Placement of proof matters as much as its content.** Phrasing: as short, dramatic and specific as possible. **Proof copy is selling copy** — must make him hungrier to read on and want the product more each line.
- **Rule: proof, like claims, is most effective when the reader unconsciously demands it and is ready to accept it as necessary and logical.** "All the rest is application."
- Each of the 4 position processes (Gradualization, Redefinition, Mechanization, Concentration) has a spot where proof does twice the work. E.g. after the bad/good spark-plug sequence built huge believability, he could have restated claims, asked for the order, or gone to the guarantee — instead he **piled authority on believability** (U.S. Air Force) → each strengthens the other. **"In copy 1 + 1 can often equal 10."** "Position increases power."
- TV ad: the manufacturer-test proof enters at **the exact moment the reader is begging for a solution** → the same stats that would be dull earlier become dramatic.
- **Documentation is inherently dull → stage it.** Build a drama in his mind where the documentation enters as the **hero** — statistics charged with emotion because they're the solution to what stands between him and what he craves.
- Definitions: **Documentation** = outside proof (stats, facts, tests). **Mechanization** = verbal/logical demonstration — proves by logic and structure alone, may use no outside data. **Verification** = arranging the documentation in the copy for maximum acceptance and emotional effect. Stop putting proof only in a headline, subhead or "Here's Proof" box — **interweave it** with promises, belief-words, momentum-builders, image-sharpeners, emotion-definers.
**Reinforcement — two claims doing the work of four:**
- First rule of all copy: **emotional impact** — every word, even proof, carries image and feeling.
- In emotional writing **1 + 1 can equal 10**: two images joined the right way can have 10x the impact of either alone. Avis: "We try harder" + its reason "We're only No. 2" → far more than the sum.
- One definition of creativity: **combining separate images into a new identity more powerful than the logical sum of its parts** — how new words, slogans, concepts and headlines are born. Do it image by image through the body copy.
- Example (presidency book): paragraph 1 openly admits flaws (first book, not an accomplished writer, "if you read for style… this is not your book") → paragraph 2: "But there are paragraphs… ideas… whole chapters… never put down on paper before that may open doors to you—tomorrow." The **juxtaposition** of frank flaw + positive claim makes the claim far more believable ("a fair report").
- **"Copywriting, in many phases, is the search for such juxtapositions."** Go back through examples and underline the junctures where one claim blends into another and sharpens it / pours strength in / makes it more believable. Build each claim on top of another.
**Interweaving — blend emotion, image and logic in the same sentence:**
- The analysis sounds mechanical; it isn't meant to be. Like a golf pro: make you consciously awkward with the new grip, then buckets of practice until it's intuitive again — **"New Awkwardness leading to New Ability."** Goal: move from Creativity #1 (raw intuition) to Creativity #2 (trained intuition) where you don't notice the devices any more than your fingers on the keys.
- From Walter S. Campbell (*Writing Non-Fiction*): no sentence works with facts alone — it needs emotion, evaluation, impact. **Every sentence should carry as much promise + image + logic + emotion as possible**, blended so you can't pull the threads apart.
  - Spark plug sentence packs 6 disadvantage images ("premium gas… 4 to 8 cents more… despite this… inefficient… wasteful… consumption"); the contrast sentence packs 5 promises ("regular gas… 8 more mpg… 31 more hp… easier starting… all weather").
  - "And, most important, [mood-builder] these experts [authority] have discovered [scientific idiom] that you do not have to be a handyman [core promise] or a mechanic [restated] in order to coax [ease] this performance [condenses all prior claims] out of your set."
  - Not done consciously — stored by practice, used intuitively.
**Sensitivity — give the reader what he demands, step by step:**
- When to stop piling promise/identification/proof? Where does he get bored, want a new image, or want the close? Where are you overselling? → **empathy: be writer and reader at once**; anticipate the point he says "I've read enough about this. Give me this instead," then shift direction.
- This is the **architecture of the whole ad**: when, where and how often you shift between copy types (promise → mechanism → documentation → promise…). Recognizing the shift points makes the ad hold together or fall apart.
- 4 book-ad structures (the ad reproductions themselves are images in the book; I read his breakdowns):
  1. **Friend-winning book:** promise (make anybody like you) → **redefine** (popularity *can be taught* — a paradox that creates a point of difference) → transition "This has been proved by…" → **documentation** (big corporations paid him to teach it) → detailed promise → documentation + price comparison → importance of the promise → documentation again → price comparison + money-back guarantee → classic transition → **long promise catalog** (now far stronger because of all the prep). Negative promises and full documentation list set **outside the main flow** for whoever wants them.
  2. **Mind-power book:** promise headline + elaboration → negative + positive redefinition → transition word ("Simply") → promise in a **definition tone** → transition in a **proof tone with promise content** → promise catalog. So blended that every word feels like pure fact.
  3. **Straight-promise ("rock-em sock-em")**: promise → elaborating promise → fundamental definition equated to a detailed promise ("then you could") → restated redefinition → sheer promise + promise-mechanism to the end. **No documentation at all** — the weight is on the momentum of the promise-train.
  4. **Beauty book:** **authority reference BEFORE the headline** (sets it apart + brings in the key persuader) → promise that also builds momentum → concentration copy (redefines what she can expect; outdates other approaches) → "Because…" → promise elaboration — but **no claim-piling, because she's skeptical and burned before**: first **agree with her skepticism**, then shift the whole story onto the doctor for 12 paragraphs → back to promise.
- **Each problem demands a different structure.** The writing signal you've lost him: "a kind of irritating confusion. A blockage… the copy has suddenly gone dead, and all you are writing now is words" — you went straight when he wanted to turn; piled promises when he stopped believing; buried him in stats when he already believed and wanted the promises again. Stop and find a new direction.
- **Post-write audit questions:** What kind of ad is this (straight promise? too much documentation? too little definition of the new world the product creates)? Does it sound like everyone else's copy? **Could you put another brand name on it and it make no difference?** Did you leave anything out? Would mechanism/believability copy, a proof section, a use photo, or a fuller "how it works" increase impact?
- **"A new structure is a new ad."** Same content + new structure = new ideas, sharper/more believable promises, even new sections. If stuck: throw away the headline and rough flow, **start 180° from where you began.** If the copy won't develop from the headline, maybe what's "begging to come out of your 6-point type" should be the headline.
**Momentum — draw him deeper:**
- Copy's first job is to be read. The longer you take to build desire/belief, the more momentum-builders you need. Two types:
  1. **Momentum phrases** in transitions (corny but they work): "Here's how", "Let me explain.", "All I ask from you is this.", "What you are going to do, in the very first hour…, is this.", "And yet it's only the beginning.", "For example—", "Read the thrilling answer below.", "Just wait till you try this." Spot them throughout long copy.
  2. **Incomplete statements / teasers** — (1) create interest in a specific point, (2) raise a question about it, (3) imply the answer comes later. ("…using nothing more than ordinary tap water, your own ten fingers, and your garden and refrigerator"; "a modern miracle… comes to life under your hood"; the device that "HAD TO BE BANNED" from European raceways for giving an "UNFAIR ADVANTAGE"; "How to grow thousands of flowers—without dirtying your hands"; "Caught! 120 fish in one hour!"; "AND THEN, NEARLY-WILD STARTED TO BLOOM!") Every one also carries sell → interweaving, no waste words.
**Mood — drama, excitement, sincerity through words and rhythm:**
- Words and rhythms are to the copywriter what line and color are to the painter; they carry emotion so subtly the reader doesn't know where it comes from.
- Two kinds of mood words: **image-sharpeners** (visual: "weighted down", "solid, blazing rows", "so thick you can't see a leaf in between") and **emotion-definers** (tell him what to feel: "precious", "that very morning", "blinded", "greets", "you never imagined before"). Emotion-definers must stay invisible — **if certain readers notice them, the copy becomes questionable or corny; if everybody sees them, it's a bad ad — rewrite.** Know your reader's sophistication **in tone as well as product awareness**; adjust tone up or down when moving to a different medium.
- **Rhythm:** read it aloud. Rich, almost blank-verse rhythm = sensuous, exciting mood (garden). **Machine-gun rhythm** = short choppy sentences, verb-verb-verb, claim-claim-claim → propels and overwhelms (mind-power letter), using **power words** ("liberate", "hold spellbound", "out-think", "tower over", "outshine") + **frank, man-to-man tone-builders** ("this letter says", "tied up inside you", "like a sponge", "stopped cold") = no nonsense, plain common sense.
- **Sincerity mood:** absence of adjectives where you'd expect them + self-kidding whimsy (VW) → affection → trust. **Deliberately clumsy, unscannable long sentences** (fire injector) → feel ingenuous and reasoned, not written by "a copy writer."
- Mood-building is deliberate for the writer, unnoticed by the reader.

# CORE SYSTEM — the whole book on one page (my synthesis)
1. **Find the mass desire** (can't create it — channel it). Score candidates on urgency, staying power, scope. Pick ONE. Watch permanent forces vs forces of change; time the trend.
2. **Analyze the product**: physical vs functional. List every performance → map each to a desire → feature the one with most power now. Physical facts = proof/justification, never the headline.
3. **Locate the market** = awareness (what they know; "readiness to accept") × sophistication (how many claims they've heard). This sets the **point of entry** for the headline: product+price / product superiority / desire / need / the market itself (identification).
4. **Headline** (5–10 words ≈ 90% of the ad's value): only job = get the next sentence read; must be interesting AND believable at first glance. Strongest claim ≠ strongest headline. Sophistication tells you claim vs enlarged claim vs mechanism vs improved mechanism vs identification. Then **verbalize** (38 ways).
5. **Body copy = alter his vision of reality** along 3 dimensions: Desire, Identification, Belief → Absolute Conviction.
6. **7 techniques:** Intensification (desire — 13 ways) · Identification (roles: character + achievement; primary image + bridges) · Gradualization (belief by structure — chain of acceptances) · Redefinition (remove objections: simplify / escalate / reduce price; concept-judo) · Mechanization (how it works: name / describe / feature) · Concentration (destroy alternatives, always with your solution) · Camouflage (borrow believability: format, phraseology, understatement, deadly sincerity).
7. **Weave:** Verification (proof placed where demanded, staged as hero) · Reinforcement (1+1=10 juxtapositions) · Interweaving (promise+image+logic+emotion in every sentence) · Sensitivity (structure shifts when the reader wants them) · Momentum (phrases + teasers) · Mood (words + rhythm).
8. **Never use a formula as the idea.** Analysis → unique product–market–timing → unique solution. Formulas only for verbalization. Every market moves; every winner goes stale.
