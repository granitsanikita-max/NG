## ② Fundamentals vs Techniques — read this first

<b>The law (prime directive):</b> every element's only job is to <b>get them to the next word.</b> Copy is a chain — one line earns the next.

- <b>Fundamentals</b> = non-negotiable. Active in <b>every</b> copy, whatever the goal. Nail these first.
- <b>Techniques</b> = the toolbox. You <b>pick</b> these based on the copy's <b>goal + awareness + sophistication</b> (Section ③). The right technique for a cold advertorial is the wrong one for a warm product page.
- <b>Copy channels desire — it never creates it.</b> The desire comes from research. Your job is to aim it at this product. <i>(BA)</i>
- <b>No formula is the idea.</b> Formulas and swipes are only for the <i>wording</i> (③E). A headline copied into a different market becomes an "echo ad" that reminds people of someone else. <i>(BA)</i>

<b>Order of work:</b> research inputs (③A) → surface (③A) → awareness + sophistication (③B–C) → headline (③E) → make every <b>fundamental</b> (④) active → layer the <b>techniques</b> (⑤) the goal calls for → chief it (⑥).

<i>Tags: <b>(BA)</b> = from Breakthrough Advertising (Schwartz) · <b>(house rule)</b> = our own rule, not from a book.</i>

## ③ Analyse the goal — do this BEFORE you write

### A. What are you writing? (surface)

<callout icon="📥" color="blue_bg">
<b>Inputs from research — bring these in, don't redo them here:</b>

1. <b>The ONE desire</b> this copy channels.
2. <b>The ONE performance</b> of the product that satisfies it best. Specs and materials are proof for it — never the hook.
3. <b>The primary image</b> — what buyers already believe this <i>type</i> of product is. Build on it; never fight it.
4. <b>The one summary trait</b> of the product's personality (e.g. "clinical", "effortless"). It leads every headline; other traits support it.
</callout>

| Surface | Its job | Reader arrives | Lean hardest on |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Product page (PDP) | Convert a visitor who already clicked | warm — interested | clarity · benefits-not-features · social proof · risk reversal · offer |
| Pre-Sales (Advertorial) | Pre-sell & warm a cold click before the store | cold / skeptical — guard up | story · curiosity & contrast · belief change · native editorial voice |
| Ad copy (native) | Look like content, earn the click | scrolling, not shopping | hook · curiosity & contrast · relatability · speed to value |

<b>Rule:</b> a PDP can be direct (they're interested); an advertorial must earn the right to sell with story / value first.

### B. Awareness guide — where are they, and what must the copy DO?

<b>Awareness = what they already know</b>: the problem → that it can be solved → that a type of product solves it → your brand → your price. It also sets how much they'll <b>accept</b>. A wall sits between each level: a headline built for one level dies at another, and stops working once the market moves up. <i>(BA)</i>

| Awareness | Headline starts with | What your copy must do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Most Aware | Product name + the deal (price, bonus, deadline). The news IS the offer. | Just the deal — price, bonus, urgency, CTA. Quick recap of top benefits. |
| Product Aware | Product name + its <b>superiority</b>, through ONE job: reinforce desire (association, sensory detail, imagery) · sharpen how it works · extend when/where it's used · new proof · new mechanism · removes an old limit · a whole new image (→ ③C) | They know your product. Kill final hesitation — proof, objections, offer, risk reversal. Picture it, prove it, show how it works. |
| Solution Aware | <b>Name the desire and/or its solution</b> so sharply they recognise it at a glance. Plain is often best; if not, add fresh proof, mystery or wonder. Product name optional. | Prove YOUR way beats the alternatives — unique mechanism, us-vs-them. Steps: name it → prove it can be done → show the mechanism is in this product. |
| Problem Aware | <b>The problem.</b> Ladder: bare problem ("Back pain?") · problem + fix · limit removed · fix + "without [feared thing]" · substitute for a costly chore ("a salon blowout in a brush") · prevention · one stressed word ("Do YOU…") · a verbal twist when the fix is over-promised | Agitate the pain, then reveal the solution + the mechanism. Dramatize it until they <i>feel</i> how badly they need it → product as the inevitable fix. |
| Unaware | <b>The market itself</b> — an identification line naming who they are or what they feel. <b>No price, no product name, no claim.</b> No product hero shot / logo in the first frame. | Don't sell. Open on the problem / a story / curiosity — make them feel the problem is real. Product comes late or not at all. Chain: identify → problem/desire real → a fix exists → this product. |

<b>Unaware — 8 ways in</b> <i>(BA)</i>: (1) name a hidden dream · (2) name a hidden fear · (3) start from an accepted image, then lead into the problem they'd reject if said bluntly · (4) voice a shared resentment · (5) show the triumph moment · (6) show the RESULT of the problem · (7) show the RESULT of the effort, not the work · (8) project the desire visually only, no words.

- <b>Rule:</b> they must recognise themselves before they can buy. It should repel as many as it attracts. A joke or pretty picture with nothing behind it = empty headline.
- <b>When to switch to Unaware:</b> spend goes up but volume drops — the product name, then the function, then the desire stop pulling. Also use it to reach people who'd never answer the direct promise.

### C. Sophistication guide — how tired is the market, and what must the copy DO?

<b>Diagnose it from research</b> (what competitors' pages and ads already say): plain claims = 1–2 · big numbers / outbidding = late 2 · "how it works" up front = 3–4 · identity, vibe, or back to plain pleasure = 5. Check body copy too, not just hooks. A new sub-category starts again at Stage 1. <i>(BA)</i>

| Stage | Market state | What your copy must do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Brand-new claim — you're first | State the need or claim plainly ("get X"). Simple works — no tricks. Dramatize it in the body, then prove it. |
| 2 | Claim still fresh, others copying | Copy the winning claim and <b>enlarge it to the limit</b> — measure it, outbid it. Can't measure the benefit? Enlarge through bigger comparisons. <i>Warning: eventually the market stops believing anyone.</i> |
| 3 | Claims stale | Introduce a NEW MECHANISM — the "how it works" no one else is saying. <b>Flip the headline:</b> mechanism + the claim shrunk to a word ("Melts fat…"). Lead = the full claim with limits removed ("…without diets, without exercise"). Must be new, legitimate, and seen as believable + significant. Only works if the desire keeps renewing. |
| 4 | Mechanism copied | Make your mechanism better / easier / faster / surer; solve more of the problem; remove a limit; add a benefit. Template: <b>"First / Only [limit removed] [mechanism]"</b>. Then: a new mechanism, or Stage 5. |
| 5 | Market burned out — believes nothing | Stop claiming. <b>Identification</b> — bring them in through who they are (⑤ Identification), or go back to the raw core pleasure stated plainly. Same playbook as Unaware. |

<b>How to be first (reset to Stage 1) without inventing anything</b> <i>(BA)</i>: sell the same product to a new market · reach the market through an untapped channel · find a performance nobody noticed and <b>coin a name for it</b>. Owning a NEW CATEGORY lives here ("fungus-killing shampoo," not "anti-dandruff").

<b>Splinter move</b> <i>(BA)</i>: own a sub-group the big players ignore, with its own mechanism + proof. Win there, then use that win to attack the main market.

### E. The headline — build it before the body

<b>1. The job</b> <i>(BA)</i>. The headline's only job is to get the next line read. It doesn't have to sell or name the product — only Most Aware + a deal sells in the headline itself. The first 5–10 words decide the ad, all or nothing.

<b>2. Build it in this order:</b>

1. <b>Entry point</b> — from ③B (what it starts with).
2. <b>Claim type</b> — from ③C: plain claim (1) · enlarged claim (2) · mechanism + one-word claim (3) · improved mechanism (4) · identity (5).
3. <b>Sharpen the wording</b> — step 4 below.
4. <b>Check the 7 ingredients</b> — step 5 below.

<b>3. Believability rules</b> <i>(BA)</i>

- It must be <b>interesting AND believed at first glance.</b> Your strongest claim is often not your strongest headline. If a big-claim hook fails, test one with fewer, more believable claims, then build up to the big claim in the body (④ #1).
- <b>Never lead with the physical product</b> (specs, materials, factory). Those prove the claim; they aren't the claim.
- ONE desire · ONE performance · ONE summary trait.
- <b>Creative gamble:</b> leaving the product out of the hook is fine — only if the hook drags them into the body.

<b>4. Sharpen the wording</b> <i>(BA — Verbalization)</i>. If you're first, the bare claim beats all of these. Otherwise, pick by purpose:

| Purpose | Use when | Ways |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <b>Strengthen it</b> | Stage 2 | measure size / amount / speed · compare · count the jobs it does ("all 5…") · before/after · remove a limit ("…without…") · borrow authority or admired people · show how much work it does |
| <b>Make it fresh again</b> | Stage 3+, or an over-promised claim | paradox ("how a bald barber saved my hair") · contradict how they think it works · metaphor · swap the product for what it replaces · a parallel reality · mechanism + claim in one line · state the difference · new / only / first · make them sense it (see, feel, hear) · one extreme example (the "electric clock" move) · torture test → "Nothing!" · how hard it was to make · split or repeat for emphasis |
| <b>Pull them into the body</b> | Unaware, creative gamble | question · challenge ("which one has it?") · how-to / "what every X should know" · offer information · a customer quote (a UGC line) · a named case story · name the problem · warn ("Don't buy another X until…") · call out who it's for, or who can't buy · "If you can [easy thing], you can [result]" · "almost too good" · challenge a limiting belief · question → answer · dramatize the result as a story moment |

<b>5. The 7 ingredients check</b> <i>(house rule — also in the Funnel & Store Page doc)</i>. Hit at least <b>4–5</b> across the H1/hook and its subhead.

| Ingredient | What it means | Fastest way to add it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Curiosity | An open loop they need closed | Hide the mechanism, a paradox, "the reason why…" |
| Call out the pain | Name the exact problem in their words | Pull the phrase from 1–2★ reviews |
| Promise the solution | The end result they get | State the outcome, not the feature |
| Specificity | Exact numbers, names, details | "22 nights", "3 straps" — not "fast", "many" |
| Simplicity | One idea, plain words | Cut to one desire; say it in one breath |
| Credibility | A reason to believe it | Authority, a test, a count, a believable (not maxed) claim |
| Timeframe | When they'll see it | "in 7 days", "the first night" |

<b>6. Headline + image</b> <i>(BA)</i>. A headline that seems to contradict its photo forces the read. Across a campaign, the core idea can't be the headline every time — rotate the angle, keep the idea.

## ④ Fundamentals — active in EVERY copy

<callout icon="✅" color="green_bg">
<b>These are non-negotiable.</b> Every piece of copy — PDP, advertorial, ad, email — must have ALL of them active. Check them in ⑥ before anything ships.
</callout>

1. ⭐ <b>Argument + Belief Architecture (the foundation).</b> Copy convinces through an airtight <b>argument</b>, not fancy words. Build a logical AND emotional case that leads to ONE conclusion — they can't help but arrive at your belief. Power words on no argument = nothing; craft the argument first, layer stronger verbs on the edit pass. Before writing, know the ONE belief they must hold to buy (<b>North Star</b>) and the step-by-step case that gets them there. Every line moves them one step down that chain — and all roads lead to your <b>unique mechanism</b> (a proprietary fix only you have, never a generic "it's amazing").
   - <i>Weak (words-first):</i> "Our AMAZING pillowcase gives you INCREDIBLE sleep!" → hype, no reason to believe, they scroll.
   - <i>Strong (argument-first):</i> "Cotton soaks up the oil + bacteria off your face all night → you press it back into your skin for 8 hours → that's why you break out no matter what serum you use. Silk doesn't absorb it. Switch the case, remove the cause." → barely any hype words; they walk themselves to the conclusion.
   - <b>Belief comes from ORDER</b> <i>(BA — Gradualization)</i>. A claim's power = its content + what prepared it. Start with something they already believe and walk them one accepted step at a time. Build a chain of yeses before the big claim; hold back the scary part (effort, price) until they're ready. One fully believed promise beats ten half-believed ones.
   - <b>You can't change a belief, only extend it.</b> Only exception: a painful, limiting belief they'd be glad to lose — with strong authority, and the new claim straight after.
   - <b>Necessary Beliefs (pre-writing).</b> Before you write a word, list the <b>MAX 6 beliefs</b> the prospect must accept to buy — each phrased as an <b>"I believe that…"</b> statement — <b>in the order they must be accepted</b> (each one rests on the last). The copy's only job is to march them to all of them, in order. If it doesn't move them toward one of the six, cut it. That order is your outline.
2. <b>The Slippery Slide.</b> Every element's only job is to get them to the next. First line = short and almost incomplete; its only job is line two.
   - <b>Momentum devices</b> <i>(BA)</i>: (a) transition phrases at each block change — "Here's why." · "Let me explain." · "But that's just the beginning." (b) Teasers — raise interest → raise a question → promise the answer later. Every teaser also carries a benefit.
3. <b>Clarity Mechanics.</b> 5th–8th grade, active voice. Repeaters (say key points twice: the term, then dead-simple + metaphor). Compression (one atomic message). <b>Make them feel smart</b> — dumb the jargon down ("kills the fungus," not "kills Malassezia"); never lecture.
   - <i>Match the grade level to the buyer (Gunning Fog Index).</i> Lower grade = wider audience; for most ads more clarity = broader appeal = more response. Cold / mass market → short sentences, simple words (\~5th–8th grade); upscale / niche → you can go bigger. Quick check on the first \~100 words: <b>(avg sentence length + % of 3+-syllable words) × 0.4 = grade level</b> (skip proper names, easy compounds like "yellowtail," and verbs that only hit 3 syllables from -ed/-es). Don't over-optimise — vary sentence length and use the bigger word when it's the right one.
4. <b>Speed to Value + Density.</b> Signal value fast; maximise value ÷ non-value; cut anything they don't need.
   - <b>Length</b> <i>(BA)</i>: as long as it takes to build desire, fit their identity, and beat their disbelief. Cut anything that does none of the three.
5. <b>Rhythm & Cadence.</b> Vary sentence length, mostly staccato.
   - <b>Rhythm carries mood</b> <i>(BA)</i>: flowing for sensory desire · machine-gun (short, verb-led, claim-claim-claim) for momentum · plain, almost clumsy for honest credibility. Read it aloud.
6. <b>Emotional Resonance.</b> Name the target emotion, screen every word for it. Fear / shock make them share; hope / relief make them buy. Buying is emotional — every element is a tuning fork. Negatives are fine only when the product resolves them (paint the problem, then the cure).
   - <b>Image-sharpeners</b> make them see it; <b>emotion-definers</b> tell them how to feel ("finally", "that very morning"). Definers must stay invisible — if the reader notices them, it reads as corny. <i>(BA)</i>
7. <b>Human Voice.</b> Write how the avatar talks; run stop-slop; read it aloud.
   - Sound native to the platform — its trust rubs off (⑤ Camouflage). Use frank, plain tone-builders ("stopped cold", "like a sponge"). Shift tone per channel — down as well as up. <i>(BA)</i>
8. <b>Anticipate & Resolve Objections (Sugarman #8–9).</b> Every objection you don't raise, the buyer raises for you — then leaves. Always paired: <b>Anticipate</b> (say the worry out loud) → <b>Resolve</b> (kill it with proof or an honest alternative). Power move: name your biggest flaw before they spot it, then flip it. Clusters: price · "will it work" · "for someone like me" · trust · ease/install · upkeep · time to results · returns/guarantee · looks. Mine 1–2★ reviews to build the list fast. Place lines at the price and right before the CTA.
   - <b>The 3 questions they ask while reading</b> <i>(BA)</i>: "Tell me more" · "Who says so?" · "How does it work?" Answer each at the moment it rises. Too hard / not important / too pricey → ⑤ Redefinition. Skeptical, burned buyers → ⑤ Skeptic Structure.
9. <b>Honesty (Sugarman's #1 rule).</b> Truth outsells hype — the customer is smarter than you think and senses a lie. Raise your flaws up front, explain why to buy anyway → trust → they buy more. "You can't fake the truth." (Pairs with #8: name the flaw, then flip it.)
   - <b>Flaw → strength, back to back</b> <i>(BA)</i>: admit a real flaw, then give the strength straight after — it reads like a fair report.
10. <b>Interweaving.</b> Every sentence carries as much <b>promise + picture + logic + emotion</b> as it can. A bare fact is dead copy. <i>(BA)</i>
11. <b>Proof placement.</b> Proof lands best the moment they'd ask "who says so?" — the same stat is dull too early, gripping right after you've made them want the answer. Stage it as the hero that removes what stands between them and the result. Short, specific, dramatic. Weave it in after the claim it backs; at a high point, top it with authority instead of rushing to the close. Secondary proof and negatives go outside the main flow (FAQ, accordions, reviews tab). <i>(BA)</i>
12. <b>The 3 Dimensions.</b> Every piece works on all three: <b>Desire</b> — they want it (⑤ Intensification) · <b>Identity</b> — it fits who they are (⑤ Identification) · <b>Belief</b> — it will work for them. Belief must prove 4 things: <b>it gives what they want · it works · people like them rely on it · nothing else does it as well.</b> Desire without belief = "sounds fake". Belief without desire = "don't care". <i>(BA)</i>
13. <b>The Consciousness Method (iceberg).</b> Copy can hit 5 depths. Each one down is harder to reach and sells harder. Reach the deepest depth the surface allows — an ad hook may touch 1–2; an advertorial or PDP should reach 4–5. <i>(Example product: posture corrector.)</i>

| Depth | What it is | What the copy does | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Conscious thought | What they'd say out loud | Say it back in their exact words, so they think "that's me" | "My back's killing me by 3pm." |
| 2. Emotion | The feeling under the thought | Name the feeling they don't say | The embarrassment of seeing yourself hunched in a photo |
| 3. Belief | What they think is true about the problem and the fixes | Bridge it (④ #1) — never fight it | "Bad posture is just how I'm built" → "it's a muscle habit, and habits can be retrained" |
| 4. Identity | Who they think they are, or are becoming | Make the product fit or upgrade that self (⑤ Identification) | From "the tired-looking one" to "the one who walks in tall" |
| 5. Consciousness (worldview) | How they experience life — the state they live in | <b>Lift & Drop</b> (below) | How it feels to move through a whole day light, open and confident |

<b>Lift & Drop — how depth 5 works:</b>

1. <b>Lift</b> — put them inside the better state. Describe what it feels like to <i>be</i> there, not the product (the morning without pain, walking into a room tall, being seen differently). Vivid enough that they feel it now.
2. <b>Drop</b> — gently bring them back to where they are today. Same moment, current reality: the stiffness, the slouch in the reflection.
3. <b>Bridge</b> — the gap between the two is what they now want closed. The product is the way back up. They don't buy the strap; they buy getting that feeling back.

<i>Rules: the lift uses their own dream (from research) · the drop is true and recognisable, never cruel · always end on the way back up.</i>

## ⑤ Techniques — pick per goal (each: good for →)

<callout icon="🧰" color="purple_bg">
<b>Pick, don't stack.</b> Use ③ (surface + awareness + sophistication) to choose the techniques THIS copy needs. Each play says when it's good for. The fundamentals (④) are still always on underneath.
</callout>

### Persuasion plays (pick by awareness / sophistication)

- <b>Belief Bridge</b> (was: Belief Change) — find the one belief blocking the buy. You don't change it; you extend it: start from the nearest belief they already hold and build steps to the one you need, with mechanism, proof, or reframe. Only contradict a painful, limiting belief — with authority, and the positive claim right after. Depth = skepticism × price <i>(house rule)</i>. <b>Good for →</b> cold / skeptical, high-price, saturated markets (heavy lift); dial down when most-aware.
- <b>Curiosity & Contrast (open loops)</b> — expectations vs reality: pain → their belief → your contrarian truth → why yours wins → one fast proof. Two builds: <b>(a) Juxtaposition</b> — pair two things that don't belong ("all-natural deodorant that performs like a toxic one"). <b>(b) Curiosity through fear</b> — a warning outpulls a tip. Guardrail: low valence / intensity — tap the emotion, don't fear-monger; leave space between the two ideas (the gap is what they read on to fill); <b>never open a loop you won't pay off.</b> <b>Good for →</b> hooks, cold ads & advertorials.
- <b>Linking (Sugarman)</b> — bridge the new to the known: a live fad / <b>trend-hijack</b>, a body part ("The Nose" = smoke detector), a familiar concept ("vitamins for your car"), quality (gold = premium), the old version, or an emotional/memory anchor. Pick POSITIVE links — a bad association backfires. <b>Good for →</b> novel / unfamiliar products, cold traffic.
- <b>Fear / loss aversion (Sugarman)</b> — make the cost of NOT acting concrete and present, not a someday risk; frame even a preventive as a real present threat. Genuine danger only; pair fear with relief/hope. <b>Good for →</b> problem-aware TOF, urgency.
- <b>Human element (Sugarman — Human Relationships)</b> — first-person conversational byline · a real story · light inoffensive humor · a <b>human hand holding a small product</b> (scale + warmth) · relatable/attractive people they'd want to belong to. <b>Good for →</b> native trust / warmth.
- <b>"That's-why" evidence</b> 🐱 — after a claim (esp. about the CAUSE of their problem), anchor it to something they already see: <b>\[claim\] → "…that's why \[the thing they already experience\]."</b> They prove it to themselves — nothing to argue with. <b>Good for →</b> mechanism / cause claims.
- <b>The silent problem</b> 🐱 — "most \[group\] already have this and don't know it — it happens silently, no obvious symptoms." Removes the need to see a symptom + expands who qualifies. True only; pair with hope. <b>Good for →</b> problem-unaware, expanding the market.
- <b>Let them reach the conclusion</b> — imply the point, don't spell it out. Give the facts, then stop before the obvious conclusion and let them connect it themselves. A conclusion they reach on their own feels like their idea and sticks harder; over-explaining makes them feel talked down to. <b>Good for →</b> skeptical / savvy readers, premium & comparison copy.
- <b>Facts over opinions (no objection surface)</b> — state a verifiable fact instead of a claim or an opinion, and there is nothing to argue with, so the objection never even forms. An opinion ("ours is the cleanest," "the best on the market") invites the reflex "sure it is"; a plain, specific fact can't be argued, so the reader draws the flattering conclusion themselves ("that's genuinely clean") and believes it, because it was their own idea. State the fact, don't sell it, then stop. Medium-agnostic — it works in an ad, on a page, in an email, on the packaging, in a video, anywhere you catch yourself asserting instead of showing. <b>Good for →</b> any surface, any market; strongest where buyers are skeptical or the category is saturated. Pairs with <b>Let them reach the conclusion</b> and <b>That's-why evidence</b>.
- <b>Communicates > says (read between the lines)</b> — a line's power is not what it states, it's what it makes the reader conclude and feel. One compressed line can carry a whole story or identity without ever spelling it out: "the dating app designed to be deleted" (you'll find love and won't need us), "you miss 100% of the shots you don't take" on a spirits brand. Say less, mean more — decide the meaning you want them to land on, then cut back to the fewest words that still trigger it. <b>Good for →</b> headlines, taglines, hero lines, end cards, any short high-impact line; and the edit pass on long copy. Pairs with <b>One Format = One Line</b> and <b>Let them reach the conclusion</b>.
- <b>Sell the cure, not the prevention (Sugarman, Axiom 15)</b> — people barely pay to <i>prevent</i> a problem they don't feel yet ("that'll never happen to me"), but pay far more, far faster, to <i>cure</i> one they already have. Frame the promise as the fix for a pain they feel RIGHT NOW, not protection against a someday risk. If the product is really a preventive, reposition it as a cure — lead with the acute problem it solves today, underplay the "avoid this later" angle (e.g. a fuel additive: "stops engine knock, saves 10% on gas," not "prevents engine problems"). <b>Exception</b> <i>(BA)</i>: prevention DOES sell when the risk falls on someone they love (kids, partner, pets). <b>Good for →</b> problem / solution-aware buyers who already feel the pain; picking the promise & headline. <b>When to use →</b> any time you're deciding what promise the copy makes — especially if the product leans preventive. If the buyer feels no present problem, don't use it raw — first make the problem feel real (pair with <b>the silent problem</b> play).

### Breakthrough plays — Schwartz's 7 techniques + extras <i>(BA)</i>

- <b>Intensification — build desire.</b> Short formats: compress into a few key words and let the campaign carry the rest. Long formats: show the ONE desire from a new angle each time — you can't repeat, but you can reinforce. The 13 angles:
  1. Full, specific description (numbers, colours, sizes)
  2. The product in action, step by step
  3. <b>Put them in it</b> — make them own it in their head: (a) put it in their hands with words; (b) sensory journey ("feel the breeze, punch the accelerator"); (c) ownership language ("when your \[product\] arrives…" / "the first day you own it"); (d) involvement device (make them DO something) <i>(was the Involvement / Ownership play — Sugarman)</i>
  4. A test they run themselves — exact steps, exact result
  5. Stretch it over time (week 1 → month 2)
  6. Other people's reactions, case stories
  7. Experts amazed
  8. Side by side with the old way
  9. The black side, then the cure (as a story, by mocking the old product, or item-by-item parallel)
  10. Ease at every touchpoint (price, use, upkeep, durability, unboxing)
  11. Metaphor / analogy
  12. Catalog close — horizontal (more uses / more buyers) or vertical (desire piled on desire)
  13. Guarantee as the climax ("…or your money back" for each result)

  <b>Stop rule:</b> cut an angle when it repeats your own phrasing or phrasing the market has already seen everywhere. <b>Good for →</b> product pages, demos, advertorials, all body copy.
- <b>Identification — build the role.</b> Two reasons to buy: the result + what owning it says about them. The product is either the <b>tool to reach</b> the role or the <b>proof they've reached it</b>. Research which roles this market accepts and which it rejects. <b>Character roles</b> (adjectives) are accepted most easily when shown, not said; <b>achievement roles</b> (titles) need a product to display them. Start from the primary image: intensify it if good; if neutral or bad, keep it, tone it down, bridge to better images. Creative brief: keep the real usage scene but glamorize every element (setting, person, props); use props that carry several roles at once; borrow cues from the product's background (clinical, pro-grade, lab). <b>Check:</b> can they see their life — or their next step up — in it? If not, open with a bridge image they instantly accept. <b>Good for →</b> supports every copy; leads in Stage 5, Unaware and look-alike products.
- <b>Gradualization devices — belief by structure.</b> Inclusion question (nearly all buyers say yes) · detailed symptom list (from research) · logic words ("Here's why", "That's because", "So what happened?") · cause → effect chain · "If… then…" · echo the authority phrase · promise → proof → promise · parallel structure (borrows acceptance) · put their symptoms inside the promise ("avoid those $15 repair bills") · keep the first promise's "how" vague until they're ready · later, restate the opening promise word for word and prove it · slip a new claim in as a continuation ("And, most important, [authority] found…"). <b>Good for →</b> advertorials, long PDPs, skeptical markets.
- <b>Redefinition — kill the 3 big objections.</b> <b>Concept-judo first:</b> the flaw becomes the proof ("smells strong" → "that's how you know it works"). <b>Too hard → simplify:</b> never lead with the hard word · state the fear as a promise ("you don't need to be techy") · rename it in easy words · shrink it to a small countable list ("just 3 steps") · isolate the truly hard part as rare ("the 5%") · compare to something familiar · shouting "EASY" fails, so show the simplicity working · revolutionary product → redefine the whole field first. <b>Not important → escalate:</b> move the benefit to a bigger desire · show that something they care about depends on it · show it matters every day. <b>Too pricey → re-anchor:</b> never "worth every penny" · say what it "would have to sell for" and repeat that anchor (per unit, per set) · it pays for itself · price per use · a small condition · "you can have it" (not "buy"). <b>Good for →</b> any product with a drawback; at the price block.
- <b>Mechanization — how much "how it works".</b> They know how this type works → <b>name it</b>, then compete on the offer. They don't, or everyone has the same mechanism → <b>describe it</b>, at the moment the promise starts to sound too good to be true — vivid and emotional, never a lecture. Saturated market → <b>put it in the headline</b>; the more mature the market, the deeper it goes (a named ingredient or process). <b>Every deal needs a cause</b> (overstock, launch, supplier deal, real price rise): lead with the cause story, bring in the bargain later, keep selling quality. <b>Good for →</b> every surface — set the amount by awareness.
- <b>Concentration — beat the alternatives.</b> Use when most buyers already use something else; skip it when you lead. <b>Never attack a weakness without fixing it in the same breath</b> — frame the attack as being for <i>their</i> good. (A) <b>Point by point:</b> their weakness → your fix, parallel sentences, weak words vs strong words, a "because" for each. (B) <b>Their story:</b> give the old way a damning label ("passive") → their lived experience step by step → "So what happened?" → the accusation inside a line they can't disagree with → "So you tried another…" → the new way. One-line version: "[Result] without [the alternative's pain]." <b>Good for →</b> comparison sections, us-vs-them, stealing customers from a leader.
- <b>Camouflage — borrow trust.</b> Match the platform's format down to small details (headline style, subheads, image style) — format alone can add \~50%. Use the phrasing that medium tells the truth in. <b>Understatement:</b> few adjectives, soft endings, make the point and stop. <b>Deadly sincerity:</b> say who it's NOT for, admit flaws. Platform style only — never fake a real outlet, byline or doctor. <b>Good for →</b> advertorials, native ads, UGC, email.
- <b>Skeptic Structure — for burned buyers.</b> Don't pile up claims. (1) Agree with their skepticism → (2) hand the story to a real authority for a long stretch (it can even sit above the headline) → (3) back to the promise. <b>Good for →</b> categories where buyers have tried and failed before.
- <b>Reinforcement — 1 + 1 = 10.</b> Pair a claim with its reason or its contrast ("We're #2, so we try harder"). Hunt for these pairings through the whole body; build each claim on the last. The flaw → strength pair (④ #9) is the main example. <b>Good for →</b> taglines, hooks, any claim that feels weak.

### Named plays (library) — <i>ACH = Adweek Copywriting Handbook · OGSL = OG Sales Letters</i>

- <b>Openings & flow:</b> Pre-headline (pull eyes to the headline) · Don't ask a "no" question (only when the answer's a guaranteed yes) · Logical flow (answer questions in order, face-to-face). <i>(First line lives in Fundamental #2; headlines in ③E.)</i> <b>Good for →</b> the top of any piece.
- <b>Keeping them reading:</b> Harmony / "Yes" ladder (nodding every line) · Comfortable buying environment (layout + first paragraphs) · Long copy that works (long beats short IF it drives action, never padding). <b>Good for →</b> advertorials, long PDPs.
- <b>The angle & the sell:</b> Sell the concept, not the product · <b>Unique mechanism</b> (the different "how it works" everything hangs off — vital in saturated markets) · Fascinations (outcome-painting bullets; diversify them). <b>Good for →</b> saturated markets, the core pitch.
- <b>Proven frameworks:</b> "I was just like you — but worse" · Halbert "If I <i><b>, then I </b></i>" open loop · Make it feel obtainable ("desperate nerd from Ohio"). <b>Good for →</b> story / founder / personal-story copy.
- <b>Psychology & state:</b> Humiliation open (start at the bottom; the climb hits hard) · The call-out (call out the exact person) · Make them believe they can obtain it · Know the emotional job first · Post-purchase (kill anxiety, then upsell) · <b>Desire to belong</b> (sell membership in the group that owns it) · <b>Sense of urgency</b> (a TRUE reason to act now — real deadline / price rise / limited quantity, never faked, placed at the end; every price cut needs a stated reason — see Mechanization). <b>Good for →</b> cold TOF, closes, upsells.

## ⑥ Chiefing — read it back as the consumer

Make sure every <b>fundamental</b> is active and every <b>technique you picked</b> is actually in there, then reread the whole thing in the buyer's lens — does it do its job?

- [ ] Named the surface (③) and used its skeleton
- [ ] Built on an argument, not hype — one belief → one conclusion
- [ ] First line is short, near-incomplete, and earns line two
- [ ] Every block earns the next; nothing deletable survives
- [ ] Matched awareness + sophistication — copy does what that stage needs
- [ ] Headline entry point matches awareness; claim type matches sophistication
- [ ] Headline is believable at first glance; hits 4–5 of the 7 ingredients
- [ ] The physical product isn't the hook
- [ ] Picked the right techniques for this surface (and they're actually in there)
- [ ] At least one real contrast / open loop that gets paid off
- [ ] Big claims come after their preparation
- [ ] Proof sits where "who says so?" would come up
- [ ] Belief proves the 4: gives what they want · works · people like them use it · nothing else does it as well
- [ ] Reached the deepest iceberg depth this surface allows
- [ ] 5th–8th grade, human voice (stop-slop passed)
- [ ] Emotion words invisible; rhythm fits the mood (read aloud)
- [ ] Native to the platform it runs on
- [ ] Claims stated as facts, not opinions
- [ ] No stock phrasing the market has already seen everywhere
- [ ] <b>Swap test:</b> put a competitor's name on it — if nothing breaks, it's not specific enough
- [ ] Objections raised + resolved (at the price and before the CTA)

## ⑦ New plays (from swipe sources) 🗂️

<i>New material only — anything already in ④/⑤ (argument-first, unique mechanism, anticipate-&-resolve objections) is deliberately not repeated here. Necessary Beliefs now lives in Fundamental #1.</i>

- <b>Specificity Rule (concretize everything).</b> Specific always beats vague — it reads as proof, not puffery. Specific numbers beat ranges: <i>"22 days"</i> > <i>"a few weeks."</i> Specific dollars beat rounded ones: <i>"\$2,700"</i> > <i>"a couple thousand."</i> <i>"I made \$97,000 in 13 days"</i> >> <i>"I made thousands."</i> On the edit pass, hunt every vague word and swap in the exact number, date, or figure. <b>Good for →</b> proof, headlines, claims — every surface.
- <b>Power Words (definition + bank).</b> Power words are <b>1–3 word phrases</b> that act as <i>heat-seeking missiles for attention.</i> When you frame a pain or an outcome, swap flat phrasing for extreme / vivid language (the <i>argument</i> stays first — this is the layer you add on the edit pass, per Fundamental #1). Keep a <b>running, performance-ranked power-word bank</b> you can hot-swap into hooks. <i>(The method for mining and building that bank lives in the Hook-Mining Method doc.)</i> If the reader notices the power word, it reads as hype. <b>Good for →</b> hooks, the edit/polish pass.
- <b>One Format = One Line.</b> Each ad format exists to deliver a <b>single line of copy.</b> If your idea needs three lines to land, it's the <b>wrong format</b> — pick the format the line fits, don't cram the line into the format. <b>Good for →</b> picking ad formats, static / short-form ads.
- <b>"The Number Is the Hook" (lists).</b> For a listicle, the <b>counted / ranked promise is itself the hook</b> — <i>"3 reasons…," "the 30 worst…"</i> — the number does the pulling. But <b>never inflate the count</b>: three real reasons beat seven weak ones. Deliver every item at full strength. <b>Good for →</b> listicle ads & advertorials, ranked-content hooks.
