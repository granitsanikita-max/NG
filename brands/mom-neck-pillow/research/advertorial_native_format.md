### Cross-article patterns (the "native grammar")

| Element | What real mom publications do | Seen in |
|---|---|---|
| Masthead | Their own wordmark + topic nav; often a promo or ad strip above | all |
| Category label / kicker | Small caps or tiny label above the H1 (LIFE, PRODUCT REVIEWS, DO NOT DISTURB) or a breadcrumb | A1, A3, A6–A10 |
| Headline | 8–15 words; question or "number + benefit" or first-person hook; commerce headlines often hedge ("Claims to…") | all |
| Dek | Always present; 1 line, conversational, sometimes a joke | all |
| Byline | Real named person + role ("Editor", "Commerce Writer", "Contributing Writer"), small round or square headshot, Updated date. **Health pieces add "Medically reviewed by [Name, credentials]"** | all |

### Voice summary
- **Person:** editorial "we" for roundups (Bump, Healthline); **first-person "I"** for single-product and editor picks (TODAY, WTE A4, Scary Mommy, Bump A2, Motherly's CNM author). The **closest format for us is TODAY A10 / WTE A4**: a first-person tester inside a light editorial frame.
- **Reading level:** about grade 7–9 (**INFERENCE**: short sentences, contractions, everyday words, puns).
- **Pain language:** comfort and mechanism ("strain", "hunching forward", "ease tension", "feel better", "without discomfort", "keeps back strain at bay"), followed by **red flags and "see your provider"**. Medical terms appear only in "types of pain" lists and in expert quotes. Even TODAY, a pillow review, hedges the headline ("Claims to Reduce Neck Pain").

## PART 2 · US legal rules for advertorials (with sources)

### 2.1 FTC Enforcement Policy Statement on Deceptively Formatted Advertisements (Dec 2015)
Source: https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/documents/public_statements/896923/151222deceptiveenforcement.pdf
- Core rule (verbatim): "advertising and promotional messages that are not identifiable as advertising to consumers are deceptive if they mislead consumers into believing they are independent, impartial, or not from the sponsoring advertiser itself."
- The **net impression** governs, and the format itself can deceive. 1968 advisory opinion: put "ADVERTISEMENT," "in clear type, sufficiently large to be readily noticed, in close proximity to the ad". **But** "the format of [an] advertisement may so exactly duplicate a news or feature article as to render the caption 'ADVERTISEMENT' meaningless and incapable of curing the deception."
- The disclosure must be in "simple, unequivocal" language. The FTC has required **"Paid Advertisement"** in infomercials and suggested **"Advertisement"** for news-format ads. "A disclosure in the text may not remedy a misleading impression created by the headline because reasonable consumers might glance only at the headline."
- **Timing:** disclosures that inform consumers "after they have clicked on and arrived at another page will not cure any misleading impression". The ad that sends traffic must not itself pose as news.
- **Materiality is presumed** for false claims that content reflects "the independent, impartial views… of ordinary consumers or experts" or "that advertising content is a news or feature article, independent product [review]…".
- The FTC assesses the ad from the **target audience's** viewpoint, here tired new moms.
- Precedents cited: a 1989 infomercial styled as "Consumer Challenge" with "investigative reporters", and supplement marketers who posed their website as "an independent, objective resource".

### 2.2 FTC "Native Advertising: A Guide for Businesses" (2015)
Source: https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/native-advertising-guide-businesses
- **Terms likely to be understood:** "Ad," "Advertisement," "Paid Advertisement," "Sponsored Advertising Content," or variations.
- **Avoid:** "Promoted" / "Promoted Stories" ("at best ambiguous"). "Presented by [X]," "Brought to You by [X]," "Promoted by [X]," and "Sponsored by [X]" may be read as merely underwriting the content, not creating it. "Company logos and names on their own are not likely adequate."
- **Placement:** "Place disclosures in front of or above the headline." On the click-into page, "as close as possible to the headline… avoid putting them far above or to the right of the headline". Near strong images, the disclosure may need to sit on the image itself. Keep disclosures when the page is shared (title tags and URLs).
- **Prominence:** label plus visual cues (shading, borders); consistent terminology.

### 2.3 FTC Endorsement Guides, 16 CFR Part 255 (revised July 26 2023, 88 FR 48102)
Sources: https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-255 · FAQ: https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/ftcs-endorsement-guides-what-people-are-asking
- **§255.0(b):** an endorsement is any message "consumers are likely to believe reflects the opinions, beliefs, findings, or experiences of a party other than the sponsoring advertiser." A persona's "my story" **is** an endorsement.
- **§255.0(f) "clear and conspicuous" (2023):** "difficult to miss (i.e., easily noticeable) and easily understandable by ordinary consumers… In any communication using an interactive electronic medium, such as social media or the internet, the disclosure should be unavoidable."
- **§255.1:** endorsements must reflect honest opinions of a **bona fide user**. "Advertisers are subject to liability for misleading or unsubstantiated statements made through endorsements." The FAQ adds: "You can't talk about your experience with a product if you haven't tried it."
- **§255.2(b) typical results:** if the endorser's results are not what people generally get, disclose "the generally expected performance". FAQ: "'Results not typical' or 'Individual results may vary' won't change that interpretation."
- **§255.2(c) actors and composites:** ads presenting "actual consumers" "should utilize actual consumers… or clearly and conspicuously disclose that the persons in such advertisements are not actual consumers."
- **§255.2(d):** do not suppress or curate reviews in a way that distorts what consumers think.
- **§255.3 experts:** an expert must actually have the expertise and must have **evaluated the product**. The Health Guidance (Example 42) adds that calling someone "Doctor" when they are not a physician, or not disclosing a paid role, is deceptive.
- **§255.5 material connections:** disclose employees, family, payment and free product.

### 2.4 FTC Rule on Consumer Reviews and Testimonials, 16 CFR Part 465 (2024, civil-penalty rule)
Source: https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-D/part-465
- **§465.2:** a business may not "write, create, or sell a consumer review [or] consumer testimonial… that materially misrepresents… (1) That the reviewer or testimonialist exists; (2) That the reviewer or testimonialist used or otherwise had experience with the product…; or (3) The… experience". This covers **invented moms, fake comments and AI testimonials.**
- **§465.4:** no paying for reviews of a particular sentiment.
- **§465.5:** insider reviews and testimonials need a disclosed relationship.
- **§465.6 company-controlled review sites (verbatim):** it is unlawful "to materially misrepresent… that a website, organization, or entity that it controls, owns, or operates provides independent reviews or opinions… about a category of… products… including… the products… it sells." **This is the rule that bans a made-up "independent" publication or review site.**
- **§465.8:** no fake follower, like or view counts.

### 2.5 FTC Health Products Compliance Guidance (Dec 2022)
Source: https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/health-products-compliance-guidance
- Health benefit claims need "competent and reliable scientific evidence", generally **randomized controlled human trials** on the product itself or an essentially equivalent one.
- "**Individual experiences aren't a substitute for scientific research.**" Satisfaction surveys don't substantiate efficacy (Example 21). Public-health advice from a medical body is not substantiation for a product (Example 22).
- **Claims about the level of evidence must be true:** "Scientists Now Agree!", "clinically tested ingredient" (Example 18), and borrowed prestige (Example 17) are deceptive if not literally supported.
- **Testimonials:** "advertisers should not make claims through consumer testimonials or expert endorsements that would be deceptive or couldn't be substantiated if the advertiser made them directly" (Example 39: insomnia-app testimonials ≠ substantiation).
- **Third-party literature (Examples 50–52):** quoting articles or studies to promote your product makes you **responsible for the claims they imply**. An advertiser-owned "independent-looking" health site must "clearly and conspicuously disclose its ownership of the site."
- Disclaimers such as the DSHEA-style "not intended to diagnose, treat…" **do not cure** an express health claim (Example 47).

### 2.6 FTC fake-news-site enforcement (2011 acai cases)
Source: https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2011/04/ftc-seeks-halt-10-operators-fake-news-sites-making-deceptive-claims-about-acai-berry-weight-loss
- The deceptive elements, verbatim from the FTC: titles like "**News 6 News Alerts**," "**Health News Health Alerts**"; "names and logos of major media outlets – such as ABC, Fox News, CBS, CNN, USA Today, and Consumer Reports" with false "**as seen on**"; "investigative-sounding" headlines ("…Miracle Diet or Scam?"); "a reporter's first-hand experience"; "**Reporters or commentators pictured on the sites are fictional**"; "The 'responses' and 'comments' following the reports are simply additional advertising content". The operators also failed to disclose their financial relationships.

### 2.7 Meta advertising policy (landing pages)
Source (verbatim, Business Help Center "Advertising policy basics checklist"): https://www.facebook.com/business/help/757209948405699
- Your website must not "contain… **misleading**, or disruptive (e.g. pop up ads) content"; it must be "clearly accessible and relevant to your ad". See **Low Quality or Disruptive Content** and **Circumventing Systems**.
- Ads must "**Accurately represent your company, product, service or brand**". They must not "Promote misleading or deceptive practices", "Make potentially misleading claims or set unrealistic expectations", use "sensationalized or exaggerated language", or run "**celebrity-bait**" content that uses the "name, likeness, or imagery of a public figure".
- Health and Wellness policy (https://www.facebook.com/business/help/2489235377779939): no ads that "imply or attempt to generate **negative self-perception** in order to promote… health related products". The brain's existing rule of no personal-attribute hooks ("your neck pain") applies to the ad.
- **INFERENCE (could not scrape verbatim; Transparency Center pages are JS-only):** Meta's Advertising Standards also cover **Prohibited Commercial Practices** (unauthorized endorsements, misleading landing experiences) and **impersonation of brands, publications or public figures**. Pages styled as fake news outlets ("Daily Health News", cloned CNN/People layouts, fake media logos) are a well-known rejection and account-restriction trigger. The safe position is the same as the FTC's: the page must look like **RecoverMama's own** content, not a third-party outlet.

## PART 3 · SPEC: how our advertorial should look to feel native and stay legal

**Format choice:** the TODAY A10 / WTE A4 template. That means a **first-person "I tried it" commerce review inside a light brand-owned editorial frame**, with The Bump's product-card grammar and WTE's red-flag/sources discipline. **INFERENCE:** this format feels native to her feed and is the easiest to keep truthful.

### Section-by-section blueprint (mobile-first, about 1,200–1,800 words)

1. **Disclosure band (very top, full width, above the masthead or just below it).** Tinted band (e.g. light lavender), 13–14px, normal weight, dark text: **"Advertisement · This article is written and paid for by RecoverMama, the maker of the pillow featured below."** It must be visible on first paint at 375px width. Do not use italic grey microtext like Romper/Scary Mommy.

2. **Masthead.** Our own wordmark: **"The Night Shift"** with the small line "a RecoverMama journal" (the name is only a suggestion; any brand-owned name works). Use a slim topic nav that leads to our real pages (e.g. Sleep · Feeding · Recovery · Shop), or no nav. **Never** use a call-letter or news-sounding name ("News 6", "Health Daily", "Mom Report"), a cloned layout of a real outlet, or another publication's colors or logo.

3. **Kicker / category label.** Tiny caps above the H1: **"ADVERTISEMENT · PRODUCT REVIEW"**. This puts the label in front of the headline as the FTC asks, and keeps a native kicker look.

4. **Headline + dek.** Serif display H1 (the lifestyle camp: Romper/TODAY/Motherly style), 8–14 words, in her words ("mom neck", "nursing hunch", "3am feed"). Hedge any outcome the way TODAY does, e.g. "I tried a contour pillow for my 'mom neck', and here's what changed (and what didn't)". One-line dek with a wink, like Scary Mommy or TODAY.

5. **Byline block.** Small square or round headshot of a **real** person · "By **[Real Name]**, [true role, e.g. 'Community Editor at RecoverMama' / 'RecoverMama customer, mom of a 4-month-old']" · "Updated [date]". Add one line under the byline: **"[Name] works for RecoverMama"** or **"[Name] received a free pillow and was paid for this story."** Show no "Medically reviewed by" unless a real credentialed reviewer actually reviewed the page and the product, with any payment disclosed. No read time (none of the real sites show one).

6. **Hero.** The author's **own phone photos** (her bed, her nursing chair, the pillow with an imprint), credited "Photo: [Name]". Lifestyle rather than studio; a split image is fine (TODAY style). No stock "woman rubbing neck at desk", no illustrations, no before/after body shots (Meta negative self-perception rule).

7. **Key Takeaways / "In a nutshell" box** (WTE/Bump style). Three bullets: what it is, who it's for, the honest catch. Example of the shape: "contour memory foam with a cutout for side sleepers · best if you wake stiff after night feeds · takes 2–3 nights to get used to the height". **INFERENCE:** the adjustment period is a likely truthful catch; replace it with real tester data before launch.

8. **Body rhythm.**
   - First-person story opening (A4/A10): the 3am feed, the hunch, what she already tried. Use the brain's failed-solutions list (stretches, Boppy, PT, chiro, heat pad) without disparaging professionals.
   - H2 every 150–250 words, phrased as **questions** (WTE/Healthline): "Why does my neck hurt more after night feeds?", "What I'd already tried", "What made this pillow different", "The first three nights", "What to consider", "Who it's best for (and who should skip it)".
   - Paragraphs of 1–4 sentences, bullets with bold leads, and one or two blockquote pull quotes (Romper A7) that are **her own real words**.
   - Pain language: comfort and mechanism only ("strain", "support", "wake up less stiff"), never "cure / treat / fix / heal".

9. **Mechanism section (the "why").** Explain posture plainly (head forward during feeds, side-sleeping gap between shoulder and mattress) with **inline linked sources**. Optionally use WTE-style "Source" chips with a numbered list at the end. Link only to sources that say what we say. Do not imply that any study tested our pillow.

10. **Expert box (optional; only if real).** Bump/TODAY "Meet the experts" pattern: name · credential · affiliation · what they evaluated · **"Paid consultant to RecoverMama"** if true. Their quotes stay general (neutral neck alignment, supporting baby instead of hunching). If we have no real expert, **cut this box.** Do not borrow a quote from a third-party article in a way that implies endorsement.

11. **Product box** (Bump/TODAY fields): product photo · **"RecoverMama Contour Pillow"** · price exactly as on the PDP (the FUNNEL/COPY rule: every number identical across ad, advertorial and PDP) · one button ("Check price at RecoverMama", not a fake retailer row) · **"What I love"** (3 bullets, true features) · **"Things to consider"** (2 bullets, real cons) · spec strip (dimensions, fill, cover, washable, trial nights, warranty) · ad label repeated in small text next to the button: "Ad · sold by RecoverMama".

12. **"What to consider / Who should skip it"** (TODAY honesty beat), plus a **red-flag line** (WTE A3): "If your neck pain spreads to your arms, comes with numbness or tingling, or follows a fall, call your doctor. A pillow is a comfort product, not a treatment."

13. **Reviews strip (only once real reviews exist).** Label it "Reviews from verified RecoverMama buyers", with real first name + initial, date and star rating, plus a link to the full unfiltered review page. If results are cited ("slept through"), show the **typical** experience next to it. Until the brain's §11 review/UGC seeding is done, **omit this section**.

14. **No comment section.** None of the 10 real sites shows public comments on articles. Use the native "Was this article helpful? Yes / No" widget instead, and only if it actually records answers.

15. **Sources list** (end of article, WTE/Healthline style): numbered, with organization · title · date · link.

16. **Author bio** (Bump/TODAY "About the writer"): a true bio, including the relationship to RecoverMama.

17. **Footer disclosures.** Repeat **"Advertisement. This page is published by RecoverMama, the seller of the product featured."** · "This content is for general information and isn't medical advice. Talk to your provider about persistent pain." · trial, return and warranty terms that match the PDP · a link to our real About page (the honest brand version per COPY.md S14) · the policy links (privacy, terms, refund) already in `brands/mom-neck-pillow/policies/`.

### Typography and colour spec (native, not cloned)
- H1: serif display (e.g. a Freight/Publico-style serif from Google Fonts), about 34–40px on desktop and 28–32px on mobile. Body: serif or humanist sans, 18px, line-height 1.65, single column at most 700px. **INFERENCE** from the screenshots.
- Kicker and byline: small caps or sans, 12–13px. Links: underlined, in the brand's dark accent.
- Palette: warm off-white page (The Bump's `#FDFCFA` feel), dark ink text, one brand accent. Use RecoverMama's own colors, **not** another outlet's signature colors (WTE purple, Healthline black/teal, Scary Mommy blush/rust).
- The disclosure band uses a tint that contrasts with the page. It must not be lighter or smaller than the byline.

### DO
- Put **"Advertisement"** above the headline and near every CTA, with "written and paid for by RecoverMama" spelled out.
- Use a brand-owned masthead name that says "RecoverMama".
- Use a real author, a true bio, the relationship disclosed, and her own photos.
- Use first-person truth, including the friction (adjustment nights, firmness, who it's not for).
- Use comfort/support language, a red-flag box and "not medical advice".
- Cite real sources inline, and only for what they actually say.
- Keep every number identical to the PDP (price, trial nights, warranty).
- Show only real reviews and the typical result, unfiltered.
- Keep the label on shares (title tag or OG title starting "Advertisement:" or "Ad:"). **INFERENCE:** this is how the FTC guide's "maintain disclosures when republished" applies to us.

### DON'T
- Invent a publication ("Mom Health News", "Parenting Daily", "News 6") or copy a real outlet's look, logo, nav or colors.
- Invent a mom, writer, reporter, doctor or physio, or use stock "Dr." headshots or white coats.
- Add a fake comment thread, fake like counts or "1,284 moms are reading this".
- Use "As seen on" or media logos unless the coverage was real and editorial.
- Claim that the pillow treats, cures, fixes, heals or prevents pain or posture, or call it "clinically proven", "doctor recommended", "physio-approved" or "studied", unless literally substantiated.
- Use unsourced stats (the "72% of breastfeeding moms" figure is barred per the brain).
- Rely on "Results may vary" to excuse an atypical result.
- Use "Presented by", "Sponsored by" or "Promoted" as the only label, or place the label only at the bottom (the Romper A7 pattern).
- Use before/after body shots or negative-self-image hooks (Meta), or "your neck pain" personal-attribute hooks in the ad.
- Use fake countdown timers, fake "was" prices or fake stock scarcity.
