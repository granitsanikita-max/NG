New Moms: Your Neck Isn't Stiff From the Feeds. It's From the 8 Hours After Them.

Postpartum physical therapists say the first year decides whether "mom neck" eases or sticks around, and the easiest change happens while you sleep.

★★★★★2,413 Ratings
By Megan Cole · Medically reviewed by Dana Reyes, DPT, postpartum physical therapist

You know the moment.

You wake up. Your eyes aren't even open yet. You go to turn your head toward the crib.

And it won't go.

Not all the way. There's a hard knot at the base of your skull. A tight band across the tops of your shoulders. Maybe a dull ache between your shoulder blades that was there yesterday too. And the day before.

So you do what every mom does. You roll your shoulders. You rub it with one hand while the baby fusses. You tell yourself it's the feeds, and it'll ease up once she's bigger.

For a lot of moms, it doesn't.

And it was never really the feeds.

First, you're not imagining this. You're not being dramatic, and you're not weak.

When researchers asked breastfeeding moms about their bodies, up to 64% said they had neck pain. That's nearly two out of every three moms. Online, moms have their own name for it. They call it "mom neck."

Most of them were told the same thing you were. It's normal. It comes with the baby. Just stretch.

Dana Reyes is a physical therapist who has worked only with new moms for 11 years. She says that advice misses the biggest part of the story.

"I see moms with this every single week," she says. "Ask any postpartum PT. We all do. And there's one question I now ask every one of them. Almost none of them have been asked it before."

I'll get to that question in a second. First, you need to see what your neck is really carrying.

Your head weighs 10 pounds. Unless you're looking down.

An adult head weighs about 10 to 12 pounds. Held straight, stacked over your shoulders, your neck carries it easily.

Tip it forward, and the weight climbs fast.

In 2014, spine surgeon Dr. Kenneth Hansraj measured exactly how fast:

Sixty pounds. That's about the weight of an 8-year-old, hanging off the back of your neck.

Now think about where your eyes go all day.

Down at the latch. Down at her face. Down at the burp cloth. Down at the diaper. And at 3am, down at your phone, because it's the only thing keeping you awake.

Most new moms feed 8 to 12 times a day, for 20 to 40 minutes at a time. Take an average day of 10 feeds at 27 minutes. That's 4 and a half hours with your head tipped forward.

That's why the base of your skull is the first thing to lock up. That's why the tops of your shoulders feel like rope. It's not weakness. It's weight.

But what surprised me most when I talked to Dana was this.

She doesn't think the feeds are the real problem.

The one question Dana asks every mom

"What do you sleep on?"

That's it. That's the question.

"Moms look at me like I'm crazy," Dana says. "They came in to talk about feeding. But look at the numbers. You spend around 4 and a half hours a day looking down. You spend up to 8 hours a night lying down."

"The night is the longest stretch your neck has. It's the one time it's supposed to rest. For most moms, it isn't resting at all."

What happens to your neck after you fall asleep

Most pillows are soft fill. Polyester, feathers, or cheap foam. They feel lovely when you first lie down. But all night, your head slowly presses them flat.

By 2am, most soft pillows have gone flat. The neck spends the rest of the night bent.

By 2am, your head has sunk. Your neck is bent again. Not forward this time, but down and sideways, with nothing under it.

If you sleep on your side, facing the baby's crib, it's worse. A flat pillow leaves a gap between your ear and your shoulder. Your neck hangs into that gap for hours. Your bottom arm gets crushed under the pillow. That's why you wake up shaking a dead arm.

Try this now

If you're lying down right now, roll onto your side, the way you sleep. Slide two fingers between your neck and the pillow. If they fit, your neck is hanging. That's where it spends the night.

Stack two pillows to fill the gap, and your head gets pushed too high the other way. Even a memory foam block has the same problem. It's one height. Right for your back or right for your side, never both.

"So she finishes a whole day of looking down," Dana says. "Then she lies down, and her neck spends up to 8 more hours bent. It never gets a single hour off. She's not recovering at night. She's adding 8 more hours."

Dana has a name for it: the Night Shift.

Your neck works the day shift, holding your head over the baby. Then it works a second shift all night, on a pillow that doesn't hold it. No breaks. No days off.

And you'd never know. You're asleep. You don't feel it happen. You only feel the result, the second you try to turn your head in the morning.

That's why it's often worst first thing, before you've even fed anyone.

Why this first year matters more than any other

This next part is why Dana says not to wait.

During pregnancy, your body makes a hormone called relaxin. Its job is to loosen your joints so your body can make room for the baby.

It doesn't switch off the day she's born. Your joints can stay looser for up to 6 months after birth, and often longer while you're breastfeeding.

"Loose is fine on its own," Dana says. "But loose joints hold whatever shape you keep putting them in. And this year, the shape you keep putting your neck in is forward and down all day, then bent all night."

"I see moms whose kids are 3 or 4 now. The stiffness never left. It just became normal for them. Some have the start of that rounded hump at the base of the neck."

That's the part nobody warns you about. Mom neck isn't always a phase. For a lot of women, it's how their neck feels for years.

"But this works both ways," Dana says. "The same looseness that lets bad habits set in means your body responds fast to good ones. The moms who change their nights early are the ones who feel it fastest."

"But I've already tried everything"

If you're reading this, you've probably tried a few things already. Moms in Dana's clinic list the same ones:

  • Massage. Feels amazing. Lasts until the next feed.
  • The chiropractor. Better for a day or two. Then $65 again next week.
  • Stretches. They help, when you remember. You rarely have a free hand.
  • The nursing pillow. Great for the feed. Does nothing for the night.
  • A new pillow from Target. Soft and lovely. Flat by week three.

None of these are bad. Massage and stretching really do help, and Dana recommends them.

But look at when they happen. In the day. For a few minutes. Then night comes, and your neck goes back to being bent for hours. Whatever the massage loosened, the night tightens again.

"It's like mopping the floor with the faucet still running," Dana says. "You're working so hard. The water just keeps coming."

So what would actually turn the faucet off?

Why most neck pillows weren't made for this

Ancient Egyptians slept on carved wooden headrests. A museum curator who tried one found the catch: it held the head, not the neck.

For thousands of years, that was the whole problem. A pillow could prop up your head. It couldn't cradle your neck.

In 1954, an inventor filed one of the first patents for a pillow shaped to fit the neck. Years later, NASA-funded researchers created memory foam to cushion test pilots. It molds to you, then slowly springs back. It doesn't go flat by 2am.

Put the shape and the foam together, and you finally get a pillow that can hold a neck in place all night.

But most of them still weren't built for a new mom.

"They're made for a guy who snores, or someone at a desk all day," Dana says. "Not for a mom who sleeps on her side facing the baby's crib, gets up three times a night, and gets milk on everything."

What Dana looks for

So Dana made a short list. If a pillow misses even one, she tells moms to skip it.

  1. It keeps its shape until morning. Slow-rebound memory foam, not fluff. If it's flat by 2am, nothing else matters.
  2. Two heights. A lower middle for your back. Higher sides for when you roll toward the baby. One height can't do both.
  3. Room for your arm. A cut-out at each side, so your bottom arm rests in front of you instead of crushed under your head.
  4. A cover you can unzip and wash. Because milk and spit-up happen. Most nights.
