# Advertising-Platform Policy & Legal/Regulatory Compliance for Marketing Women's Leak-Proof / Incontinence Underwear (2026)

Scope note: US primary, with brief UK/EU (ASA/MHRA) notes. "As of 2026." Platform policies change frequently; every claim below is sourced and should be re-verified against the primary policy page before launch. This is research synthesis, not legal advice.

## Q1. Meta & TikTok ad policy: personal-health / personal-attributes / sensitive-condition rules; triggering copy vs. safe framing

### Takeaway
Meta will not reject you for *selling* incontinence underwear, but it will reject copy (and image/video text) that asserts or implies it knows the *viewer* has incontinence — the "you suffer from incontinence?" construction is the single highest-risk pattern. The fix is framing (describe the product / "people who want X" / shared-experience confidence messaging), not targeting changes. TikTok's US rules are lighter on content specifics but still bar medical/treatment claims in many markets and require 18+ targeting for health categories.

### Cited Findings — Meta
- Meta's **Personal Attributes policy** prohibits ads that "assert or imply" knowledge of a viewer's protected characteristics, which explicitly include "physical or mental health (including medical conditions)." Incontinence is a medical condition and is therefore squarely covered. — [Meta Transparency Center](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/objectionable-content/privacy-violations-personal-attributes/)
- The policy "largely discourages second-person language in ad copy" (e.g., "Conquer your depression") — the "you + protected attribute" combination is the trigger. — [Accelerated Digital Media 2026 guide](https://www.accelerateddigitalmedia.com/insights/guide-to-social-media-health-ad-restrictions-2026/)
- The practical reviewer test is the **"you" test**: copy risks rejection when second-person wording implies the reader belongs to a protected category. Copy about the product, the brand, or a general group is usually fine; copy that *assigns* the attribute to the reader is not. — [Stackmatix: Meta Ads Personal Attributes Policy](https://www.stackmatix.com/blog/meta-ads-personal-attributes-policy)
- The policy "applies to images and video text too, not just the written body copy," and "'implied' is enough" — you need not state the attribute outright; suggesting it through "you" triggers the same outcome. On-image overlays, captions and spoken video words all count. — [Stackmatix](https://www.stackmatix.com/blog/meta-ads-personal-attributes-policy)
- Concrete rejected→compliant pattern (illustrative, Stackmatix's invented examples): "You suffer from anxiety and can't sleep" (REJECTED — assigns condition to reader) → "We publish guided sleep routines for anyone dealing with stress" (COMPLIANT). The compliant rewrites never name the reader; they describe the offer or a broad group in third person. — [Stackmatix](https://www.stackmatix.com/blog/meta-ads-personal-attributes-policy)
- Meta's broader **Health and Wellness advertising standard** is enforced against *what an ad claims*, not what it depicts. Ads can't make "claims … to cure, heal, or eliminate" listed incurable diseases, but Meta is explicit that "this does not apply to claims about treating or managing symptoms" — i.e., managing symptoms is sayable, curing is not. — [ADM 2026 guide](https://www.accelerateddigitalmedia.com/insights/guide-to-social-media-health-ad-restrictions-2026/), citing [Meta Health & Wellness standard](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/restricted-goods-services/health-wellness/)
- The Health & Wellness standard prohibits "statements of inferiority about physical appearance" — negative terms/questions that "attack an individual's appearance, specific body parts, or hygiene." Shame-based incontinence hooks ("embarrassing leaks," "smell") risk this. — [ADM 2026 guide](https://www.accelerateddigitalmedia.com/insights/guide-to-social-media-health-ad-restrictions-2026/)
- **Dietary/health/weight-loss products must be targeted to 18+** on Meta. (Underwear is apparel, so this specific age gate may not apply, but health-adjacent creative draws scrutiny.) — [ADM 2026 guide](https://www.accelerateddigitalmedia.com/insights/guide-to-social-media-health-ad-restrictions-2026/)
- Enforcement tightening into 2026: Meta flags/disables **custom & lookalike audiences and custom conversions** whose names/rules/metadata imply sensitive traits (e.g., an "incontinence_interest_list" audience or "appointment booked" conversion). Advertisers should use neutral audience/conversion naming; note that neutral naming avoids *automated* disablement but "does not make the list eligible" — the underlying data must have a lawful basis. — [ADM 2026 guide](https://www.accelerateddigitalmedia.com/insights/guide-to-social-media-health-ad-restrictions-2026/)
- Health/wellness brands are categorized and may receive partial or full **conversion-event optimization restrictions** (losing lower-funnel optimization like purchases). Partial restriction applies to most health brands. — [ADM 2026 guide](https://www.accelerateddigitalmedia.com/insights/guide-to-social-media-health-ad-restrictions-2026/)

### Cited Findings — TikTok
- TikTok's health policies are **country-specific**; in the US, health-category advertising must target users **18+**. — [ADM 2026 guide](https://www.accelerateddigitalmedia.com/insights/guide-to-social-media-health-ad-restrictions-2026/)
- **Medical devices and OTC products** may be advertised on TikTok if they "comply with all local laws and regulations and have FDA approval where necessary." (Relevant if the product is framed/classified as a device — see Q3.) — [ADM 2026 guide](https://www.accelerateddigitalmedia.com/insights/guide-to-social-media-health-ad-restrictions-2026/) citing [TikTok Healthcare policy](https://ads.tiktok.com/resources/help/article/tiktok-ads-policy-healthcare-pharmaceuticals)
- TikTok's prohibition on "medicinal claims to treat, cure, heal, or prevent any medical condition, disease, or symptoms" is stated in 14 named markets and is **not** in the US row of TikTok's policy matrix; "claims to … treat hair loss"-type performance claims appear in 44–46 named markets, again not US. So US TikTok is comparatively permissive on content, but EU/other markets are stricter — check the destination-market row. — [ADM 2026 guide platform matrix](https://www.accelerateddigitalmedia.com/insights/guide-to-social-media-health-ad-restrictions-2026/)
- TikTok, like Meta, asks advertisers not to use its tracking technology to collect healthcare information. — [ADM 2026 guide](https://www.accelerateddigitalmedia.com/insights/guide-to-social-media-health-ad-restrictions-2026/)

### Inferences
- Safe framing that satisfies the "you" test: shared-experience/aspirational confidence copy ("Stay confident and dry, all day," "Underwear designed for leaks — so you don't think about them," "Made for women who want worry-free protection"). These describe the product/outcome or a self-selecting group ("women who want X") rather than telling the reader she *has* incontinence.
- Highest-rejection-risk constructions to avoid: "Do you suffer from incontinence?"; "Struggling with bladder leaks?"; "Tired of embarrassing leaks and odor?"; any second-person diagnosis. Move condition-qualification to the landing page or quiz where the user self-selects.
- Because the rule covers image/video text, on-screen captions in UGC-style TikTok/Reels creative must be screened to the same standard, not just the body copy.

### Gaps
- Meta's policy pages were returned as excerpts (I did not fetch the full raw Meta page); exact current clause wording should be re-read at the Meta Transparency Center URLs before launch.
- No incontinence-specific Meta/TikTok enforcement example was found; findings are from the general policy text and a 2026 practitioner guide.

## Q2. FTC: substantiation for performance claims; Everdries-style medical claims; fake reviews & fabricated urgency

### Takeaway
"Leakproof," "holds X ml," "odor-proof," "100% leak-proof" are objective performance claims requiring "competent and reliable scientific evidence" before they run (absolute claims like "100%" and "medical-grade" are the riskiest). Medical claims (prevents UTIs, recommended by gynecologists, treats incontinence) require human clinical substantiation and can push the product toward FDA device status. The FTC's 2024 Fake Reviews Rule makes fabricated reviews, undisclosed insider reviews, and fake urgency independently penalizable (civil penalties per violation).

### Cited Findings — substantiation standard
- The FTC issued its **Health Products Compliance Guidance on December 20, 2022**, replacing the 1998 Dietary Supplement guide and broadening it to **all health-related claims**. — [Covington](https://www.cov.com/en/news-and-insights/insights/2023/01/ftc-issues-new-guidance-on-health-related-claims-to-replace-the-dietary-supplements-advertising-guide); [Jones Day](https://jonesday.com/pt/insights/2023/01/ftc-overhauls-and-expands-25yearold-health-products-advertising-compliance-guide)
- Objective product claims must be substantiated **before dissemination** by "competent and reliable scientific evidence." For health-benefit/efficacy claims the FTC treats **randomized, controlled human clinical trials (RCTs)** as the most reliable; animal/in-vitro studies and consumer surveys generally cannot substantiate a health claim on their own. — [Covington](https://www.cov.com/en/news-and-insights/insights/2023/01/ftc-issues-new-guidance-on-health-related-claims-to-replace-the-dietary-supplements-advertising-guide)
- Required substantiation depends on the type of product/claim, cost/feasibility, consequences of a false claim, and what experts in the field consider reasonable. Vague qualifiers ("helps," "may," "promising," "preliminary") are **likely inadequate** to cure an otherwise unsupported claim. — WebSearch synthesis of [McDermott](https://www.mwe.com/?p=222864) and [Jones Day](https://jonesday.com/pt/insights/2023/01/ftc-overhauls-and-expands-25yearold-health-products-advertising-compliance-guide)
- Advertisers are liable for misleading **endorsements/testimonials** and cannot use a testimonial to make a claim they could not substantiate directly. — [Covington](https://www.cov.com/en/news-and-insights/insights/2023/01/ftc-issues-new-guidance-on-health-related-claims-to-replace-the-dietary-supplements-advertising-guide)
- The FTC's own guidance document is linked by Meta's practitioner guidance as the standard that landing pages must satisfy ("the destination substantiates every claim the ad makes, and adds none"). — [ADM 2026 guide pre-launch checklist](https://www.accelerateddigitalmedia.com/insights/guide-to-social-media-health-ad-restrictions-2026/) citing [FTC Health Products Compliance Guidance](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/health-products-compliance-guidance)

### Cited Findings — Everdries-style claims (risk assessment)
- "Medical-grade," "prevents UTIs," "recommended by gynecologists" are all **health/disease claims** that (a) require human clinical substantiation under the 2022 guidance, and (b) may convert the product into an FDA-regulated device by virtue of the claim (see Q3). "Recommended by gynecologists" additionally requires the endorsement to be truthful, from real endorsers, reflecting their honest views (FTC Endorsement Guides) — the Everdries-style ecosystem has instead used *fabricated* doctor endorsements (see Q5).
- Everdries itself carries a **2.5–2.9 / 5 Trustpilot rating** with widespread complaints that the product "doesn't work as advertised" and does not hold leaks even for light incontinence — illustrating the gap between "leakproof"/"all day" marketing and performance, which is exactly the false-advertising exposure. Note: Trustpilot reviews are individual opinions, not verified findings. — [Trustpilot everdries.com](https://www.trustpilot.com/review/everdries.com)

### Cited Findings — Fake reviews & fabricated urgency
- The FTC announced its **final Rule on the Use of Consumer Reviews and Testimonials on August 14, 2024** (effective ~60 days after Federal Register publication; practitioner sources put the effective date in late October 2024 — confirm against the Federal Register). — [Hunton](https://hunton.com/hunton-retail-law-resource/ftc-issues-final-rule-targeting-fake-consumer-reviews-and-testimonials-including-those-generated-by-artificial-intelligence-ai-and-online-bots); [Manatt](https://manatt.com/insights/newsletters/client-alert/ftc-issues-final-rule-banning-fake-reviews-and-tes)
- The rule prohibits: buying/selling fake reviews and testimonials (including **AI- and bot-generated** ones); company-controlled "independent" review sites; review suppression via threats/intimidation; fake social-media indicators (bot followers); and **insider reviews without clear disclosure** of the relationship. — [Hunton](https://hunton.com/hunton-retail-law-resource/ftc-issues-final-rule-targeting-fake-consumer-reviews-and-testimonials-including-those-generated-by-artificial-intelligence-ai-and-online-bots)
- The FTC can seek **civil penalties per violation** where there is actual or implied knowledge, and may treat each fake review as a separate violation. Reported per-violation figures conflict across sources (~$50,120 vs. ~$51,744; the higher is the inflation-adjusted figure) — verify the current amount. — WebSearch synthesis of Manatt/Hunton/Retail Insight
- Fabricated urgency / fake countdown timers / false scarcity are deceptive-format problems the FTC pursues as unfair/deceptive acts and as "dark patterns"; the native-advertising/deception framework asks whether reasonable consumers are misled about an offer's nature. — [Hunton native advertising guidance](https://hunton.com/hunton-retail-law-resource/ftc-issues-guidance-on-native-advertising-businesses-must-consider-the-likelihood-of-consumer-confusion)

### Inferences
- Rank claims by risk for this product: (lowest) "leak-resistant," "absorbs up to X ml in lab testing," "helps you stay dry" → (higher) "leakproof," "odor-control" → (highest/avoid unless clinically backed) "100% leakproof," "medical-grade," "prevents UTIs," "doctor/gynecologist recommended," "treats incontinence."
- "Holds up to X ml" is defensible only with documented absorbency testing (and should say "up to," define the test conditions, and not over-promise real-world all-day use). "100%"/absolute claims invite a "every reasonable interpretation must be true" reading and are hard to substantiate.
- A static countdown timer that resets on refresh, or "only 3 left" that never changes, is a classic FTC dark-pattern/false-urgency target and should be avoided or made genuinely dynamic.

### Gaps
- Exact current FTC per-violation penalty figure and confirmed Federal Register effective date for the Fake Reviews Rule were not nailed down from primary source; practitioner sources conflict.
- No FTC enforcement action specifically against a leak-proof/period-underwear brand was located (Everdries evidence is consumer reviews, not a regulator action).

## Q3. FDA: is leak-proof / incontinence underwear a regulated medical device? What turns it into one? Labeling.

### Takeaway
A **protective incontinence garment** is a Class I, 510(k)-exempt medical device (21 CFR 876.5920, product code EYQ). Reusable absorbent underwear marketed for incontinence falls in this bucket. Menstrual (period) underwear's status is unsettled — FDA has clearly classified pads (Class I) and tampons (Class II) but has not squarely addressed period underwear in the CFR. The key lever is the *claim*: apparel/absorbency positioning stays a consumer product; disease/treatment/UTI-prevention claims pull it into device regulation (and device claims must be substantiated/cleared).

### Cited Findings
- FDA classifies "**Garment, Protective, For Incontinence**" under **21 CFR 876.5920** as a **Class I** device, submission type **510(k) exempt**, product code **EYQ**, and exempt from most GMP requirements (apart from record-keeping/complaint files) provided it is not labeled sterile. The regulation covers a device with absorbent padding and a fluid barrier to protect a patient's clothing from excreta; it excludes infant diapers. — [FDA product classification (EYQ)](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/scripts/cdrh/cfdocs/cfPCD/classification.cfm?ID=EYQ); [RegDataLab EYQ summary](https://www.regdatalab.com/device-class/EYQ)
- Historical 510(k) clearances (e.g., P&G Attends incontinent undergarment, 1985) sit under this code, confirming reusable/disposable incontinence garments are treated as Class I devices when marketed for incontinence. — WebSearch synthesis of FDA 510(k) records
- For **menstrual** products: menstrual pads are generally Class I (several narrowly defined exceptions) and exempt from 510(k); menstrual **cups** are Class II but 510(k)-exempt; **tampons are Class II and require a 510(k)**. — [Frontiers in Reproductive Health review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/reproductive-health/articles/10.3389/frph.2023.1224421/full)
- **Period underwear is not listed** alongside tampons/pads/cups as a medical device in the CFR, so its device status is ambiguous; one seller's attorney reportedly advised no 510(k) is needed, while Amazon's own menstrual-device policy may request 510(k) documentation. Treat as unsettled. — [Popular Science](https://www.popsci.com/story/health/pfas-period-underwear-toxic-chemicals/); WebSearch synthesis
- FDA has 2005 guidance on 510(k) submissions for menstrual tampons and pads (device framework for the category). — WebSearch synthesis ([FDA menstrual device guidance index](https://www.fda.gov/MedicalDevices/ucm071781.htm))

### Inferences
- A product marketed purely as apparel/absorbent underwear ("leak-proof underwear," "stay dry," "for light bladder leaks" framed as comfort/confidence) can likely stay in the Class I / 510(k)-exempt lane or outside active device regulation — but registration/listing obligations for a Class I device may still apply if it meets the "garment, protective, for incontinence" definition. Confirm with FDA/CDRH or regulatory counsel.
- **Claims that likely trigger device regulation or heightened scrutiny**: "prevents/reduces UTIs," "treats/cures incontinence," "medical-grade," "therapeutic," "clinically proven to manage bladder conditions," any diagnosis/treatment language. "Prevents UTIs" is a disease-prevention claim — the most dangerous for both FDA and FTC.
- Labeling constraints: if sold as a Class I incontinence device, avoid "sterile" unless validated; keep complaint files/records; don't make unapproved disease claims. If sold as apparel, avoid any labeling that would recharacterize it as a device.

### Gaps
- No definitive FDA statement on reusable *period/menstrual* underwear's classification was found; status genuinely appears unsettled in 2026.
- Whether a Class I incontinence-garment maker must register/list with FDA (vs. pure exemption) wasn't confirmed from a primary FDA page.

## Q4. PFAS / materials liability: Thinx precedent, state PFAS laws, substantiating "PFAS-free / non-toxic"

### Takeaway
The Thinx class action is the controlling cautionary precedent: a ~$5M settlement over *marketing* (sustainable/non-toxic/organic positioning) while PFAS were allegedly detectable. California (AB 1817) and New York now ban intentionally-added PFAS in apparel/textiles (effective Jan 1, 2025), so PFAS is both a liability and a now a legal-compliance issue. "PFAS-free"/"non-toxic" claims must be substantiated with testing (total organic fluorine / targeted PFAS testing) or they become their own false-advertising exposure.

### Cited Findings — Thinx
- **Dickens v. Thinx Inc.** (S.D.N.Y., No. 1:22-cv-4286-JMF): class covers purchasers of Thinx Period Underwear **Nov. 12, 2016 – Nov. 28, 2022**; allegations concerned short-chain PFAS, Agion antimicrobial treatment, and the organic-cotton line. — [Thinx settlement site](https://www.thinxunderwearsettlement.com/)
- Settlement value **up to $5 million**; eligible claimants could receive about **$150** (or a product voucher); **final approval June 8, 2023**; claim deadline April 12, 2023. Thinx **denied all allegations** and stated PFAS "have never been a part of its product design." — [Thinx settlement site](https://www.thinxunderwearsettlement.com/); [Consumer Notice](https://www.consumernotice.org/environmental/water-contamination/pfas/thinx-underwear/); [Sierra Club](https://www.sierraclub.org/sierra/ask-ms-green/what-thinx-class-action-settlement-means-all-us)
- The suit was framed as being "related to how products were marketed (not about injuries or harm caused by the" product) — i.e., it was a **false-advertising/marketing** case, not a personal-injury case. This is the key lesson: the legal risk was the *non-toxic/sustainable claim*, not just the chemistry. — [Thinx blog](https://www.thinx.com/blogs/periodical/our-commitment-to-product-safety-at-thinx); [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2023/01/19/1150023002/thinx-period-underwear-lawsuit-settlement)

### Cited Findings — State PFAS laws
- **California AB 1817** took effect **January 1, 2025**: bans manufacture/distribution/sale of new textile articles with "regulated PFAS." Broad scope (apparel, accessories, handbags). Manufacturers must provide sellers a compliance certificate certifying no intentionally-added PFAS **or** total organic fluorine ≤ **100 ppm**. Outdoor apparel for severe wet conditions has a later start (Jan 1, 2028) with earlier disclosure. — [Morgan Lewis](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2024/11/new-york-and-california-bans-on-pfas-in-textiles-and-apparel-begin-january-1-2025); [Venable](https://www.venable.com/insights/publications/2024/10/fashion-industry-beware-pfas-bans-on-apparel)
- **New York** parallel ban also effective **January 1, 2025**: prohibits sale/offer-for-sale of new apparel with intentionally-added PFAS (apparel only; narrow exclusion for protective professional uniforms). — [Morgan Lewis](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2024/11/new-york-and-california-bans-on-pfas-in-textiles-and-apparel-begin-january-1-2025)
- **Minnesota** (Amara's Law) ban effective same date for several product categories (fabric treatments, children's products, textile furnishings, upholstered furniture). Colorado phasing in outdoor-apparel restrictions with a disclosure step. — [FashionUnited](https://fashionunited.uk/news/business/what-to-know-states-banning-or-prohibiting-pfas-in-textiles-in-2025/2025011579575)

### Inferences
- Any brand selling in CA or NY from 2025 onward must ensure no intentionally-added PFAS and keep supplier compliance certificates / TOF (total organic fluorine) and targeted PFAS test reports. This is a hard legal requirement, independent of advertising.
- "PFAS-free," "non-toxic," "clean," "chemical-free," "organic," and "sustainable" claims should be treated as substantiation-bearing claims: back them with lab testing (TOF screen plus targeted PFAS panel), name the standard/threshold, and keep documentation. Over-broad "non-toxic"/"chemical-free" claims are exactly what drew the Thinx suit and what plaintiff firms and the FTC's Green Guides scrutinize.
- Antimicrobial/odor treatments (e.g., silver/Agion) were also part of the Thinx allegations — vet any antimicrobial finish both for claim substantiation and for EPA/FIFRA pesticide-registration issues if odor/antibacterial claims are made.

### Gaps
- No source confirmed whether any state PFAS statute specifically names menstrual/period underwear as a covered or excluded category; apparel bans appear to capture it, but confirm product-category definitions.
- 2026-specific new state PFAS laws were not enumerated; the research captured the 2025 CA/NY/MN wave. Additional states likely added restrictions — verify current multistate status.

## Q5. The fake-doctor advertorial tactic (real doctors' likeness misused) — legal risk

### Takeaway
Using a real doctor's image/voice/likeness (including AI deepfakes) to fabricate an endorsement is multi-front illegal exposure: FTC Endorsement Guides + the 2024 Fake Reviews Rule (fabricated testimonials), Lanham Act §43(a) false endorsement, state right-of-publicity/misappropriation claims by the doctor, and FTC deceptive-format/native-advertising liability if the ad masquerades as editorial. This is the signature tactic of the scammy end of this category and must be entirely avoided.

### Cited Findings
- The exact tactic is documented in this product category: fact-checkers found Facebook videos advertising "magnetic underwear" that **reused the vlogs of real doctors** (cardiologist Willie Ong and Dr. Krizzle Luna) to fabricate endorsements, splicing unrelated medical content to imply the doctors backed the product. — [VERA Files: videos deceptively use doctors' vlogs to promote magnetic underwear](https://verafiles.org/articles/vera-files-fact-check-videos-deceptively-use-doctors-vlogs-to-promote-magnetic-underwear)
- The same fabricated-doctor pattern recurs for intimate/feminine products: impostor pages falsely used Dr. Liza Ramoso-Ong to "endorse" an unregistered vaginal perfume, with a fabricated doctor-with-patient photo. — [VERA Files: Liza Ong not endorsing vaginal perfume](https://verafiles.org/articles/vera-files-fact-check-liza-ong-is-not-endorsing-an-unregistered-vaginal-perfume)
- A global AFP report notes fraudsters routinely impersonate doctors and fabricate their comments/endorsements to push bogus products — a recognized worldwide pattern, not isolated incidents. — [Malay Mail / AFP](https://www.malaymail.com/news/world/2023/06/30/significant-threat-around-world-doctors-battle-impersonators/77136)
- FTC **Endorsement Guides**: endorsements must reflect the honest opinions/findings/experience of the endorser — a fabricated or non-consensual endorsement is deceptive per se. — WebSearch synthesis (FTC Endorsement Guides, via [Fenwick](https://www.fenwick.com/insights/publications/ftc-issues-guidance-on-claims-about-ai-powered-products))
- The 2024 **Fake Reviews Rule** independently bans fake/false testimonials, including AI-generated ones. — [Hunton](https://hunton.com/hunton-retail-law-resource/ftc-issues-final-rule-targeting-fake-consumer-reviews-and-testimonials-including-those-generated-by-artificial-intelligence-ai-and-online-bots)
- FTC native-advertising guidance: ads formatted as editorial must be identifiable **as ads before the consumer clicks through** (labels like "Ad"/"Sponsored"); deception is judged on the totality of presentation and likelihood of consumer confusion. Fake-news-style advertorials ("Local gynecologist reveals…") violate this. — [Hunton native advertising guidance](https://hunton.com/hunton-retail-law-resource/ftc-issues-guidance-on-native-advertising-businesses-must-consider-the-likelihood-of-consumer-confusion)
- On AI: if simulated content showing product performance could reasonably be taken as real, the advertiser must clearly disclose it is simulated; AI-generated likenesses need consent and disclosure. — WebSearch synthesis ([Chambers Advertising & Marketing 2025](https://gpg-pdf.chambers.com/Advertising-and-Marketing-2025/341/))

### Inferences
- Beyond FTC exposure, a misused doctor has private claims: **Lanham Act §43(a)** false endorsement (likelihood of confusion as to sponsorship) and **state right-of-publicity / misappropriation of likeness** (statutory in CA, NY and many states), plus defamation/false-light risk. These generate private damages independent of any regulator.
- An "advertorial" styled as a news article or an organic doctor's vlog is a deceptive format even if the doctor is fictional — undisclosed ad-nature is itself the violation. Any testimonial must be a real, consenting user/clinician reflecting genuine experience, clearly disclosed as an ad/paid endorsement, and substantiated.

### Gaps
- No US-specific regulator enforcement action against a *leak-proof underwear* fake-doctor ad was located; the documented cases are Philippine (VERA Files) and a global AFP pattern, applied here to the same product niche.
- A specifically-named VERA Files *incontinence* underwear fact-check was not found (the closest is the "magnetic underwear" doctor-vlog case).

## Q6. Concrete DO-SAY / DON'T-SAY list (ad copy + landing pages)

### Takeaway
Keep copy product- and confidence-focused, qualify audiences on the landing page not the ad, substantiate every performance number, and never use condition-diagnosis "you" language, absolute/medical claims, fabricated endorsements, or fake urgency.

### DO-SAY (lower risk; still substantiate performance numbers)
- "Stay confident and dry" / "All-day confidence" / "Worry-free protection" — outcome/confidence framing. (Satisfies Meta "you" test.) — [ADM](https://www.accelerateddigitalmedia.com/insights/guide-to-social-media-health-ad-restrictions-2026/); [Stackmatix](https://www.stackmatix.com/blog/meta-ads-personal-attributes-policy)
- "Leak-resistant underwear designed for women who want extra protection" — describes product + self-selecting group ("women who want X"), not the reader's condition. — [Stackmatix](https://www.stackmatix.com/blog/meta-ads-personal-attributes-policy)
- "Absorbs up to X ml (lab-tested)" with defined test conditions — only if you hold the absorbency test data. — [FTC Health Products Compliance Guidance](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/health-products-compliance-guidance)
- "Helps manage light bladder leaks" (symptom-management framing, allowed where "cure" is not) — Meta permits symptom-management language. — [ADM](https://www.accelerateddigitalmedia.com/insights/guide-to-social-media-health-ad-restrictions-2026/)
- "PFAS-free, verified by third-party testing" — only with TOF + targeted PFAS test documentation on file. — [Morgan Lewis](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2024/11/new-york-and-california-bans-on-pfas-in-textiles-and-apparel-begin-january-1-2025)
- Real, consenting, disclosed customer testimonials reflecting genuine experience. — [FTC Fake Reviews Rule](https://hunton.com/hunton-retail-law-resource/ftc-issues-final-rule-targeting-fake-consumer-reviews-and-testimonials-including-those-generated-by-artificial-intelligence-ai-and-online-bots)
- Move condition/severity qualification to the landing-page quiz or product selector (user self-selects). — [Stackmatix](https://www.stackmatix.com/blog/meta-ads-personal-attributes-policy)

### DON'T-SAY (high rejection / legal risk)
- "Do you suffer from incontinence?" / "Struggling with bladder leaks?" / "Tired of embarrassing leaks?" — implies viewer's medical condition (Meta Personal Attributes rejection; also appearance-inferiority risk). — [Meta](https://transparency.meta.com/policies/ad-standards/objectionable-content/privacy-violations-personal-attributes/); [Stackmatix](https://www.stackmatix.com/blog/meta-ads-personal-attributes-policy)
- "100% leak-proof" / absolute guarantees — hard to substantiate; invites literal-truth scrutiny. — [FTC HPCG](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/health-products-compliance-guidance)
- "Medical-grade," "therapeutic," "clinically proven to treat incontinence" — unsubstantiated disease/efficacy claims; may trigger FDA device status. — [FDA EYQ](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/scripts/cdrh/cfdocs/cfPCD/classification.cfm?ID=EYQ); [FTC HPCG](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/health-products-compliance-guidance)
- "Prevents UTIs" / "cures/eliminates incontinence" — disease-prevention/cure claims (FDA + FTC). Meta bars cure claims for listed conditions. — [ADM](https://www.accelerateddigitalmedia.com/insights/guide-to-social-media-health-ad-restrictions-2026/)
- "Recommended by gynecologists" / any doctor endorsement that isn't a real, consenting, substantiated endorsement — Endorsement Guides + Fake Reviews Rule + §43(a)/right of publicity. — [VERA Files](https://verafiles.org/articles/vera-files-fact-check-videos-deceptively-use-doctors-vlogs-to-promote-magnetic-underwear); [Hunton](https://hunton.com/hunton-retail-law-resource/ftc-issues-final-rule-targeting-fake-consumer-reviews-and-testimonials-including-those-generated-by-artificial-intelligence-ai-and-online-bots)
- "Non-toxic" / "chemical-free" (bare, unqualified) — Thinx-style exposure. — [NPR/Thinx](https://www.npr.org/2023/01/19/1150023002/thinx-period-underwear-lawsuit-settlement)
- Fabricated urgency: fake countdown timers, "only 3 left" that never changes, fake "news article" advertorials. — [Hunton native advertising](https://hunton.com/hunton-retail-law-resource/ftc-issues-guidance-on-native-advertising-businesses-must-consider-the-likelihood-of-consumer-confusion)
- Before/after "leak" imagery or shame/hygiene-inferiority hooks — Meta appearance-inferiority rule. — [ADM](https://www.accelerateddigitalmedia.com/insights/guide-to-social-media-health-ad-restrictions-2026/)

## UK / EU differences (brief)

### Takeaway
UK rules (ASA/CAP + MHRA) are stricter on "medicinal claims": framing incontinence underwear as relieving/treating a condition can make it an unlicensed medicinal/device claim, and testimonials and statistics must be documented.

### Cited Findings
- ASA upheld a complaint against "VCARE Prostate Therapy Underwear": claims to relieve symptoms of, cure, or heal a specific condition are **medicinal claims**, and urinary incontinence/urine-flow problems were treated as symptoms of adverse medical conditions. Medicinal claims may only be made for an **MHRA-licensed medicinal product or a medical device** (CAP rule 12.1). — [ASA ruling (VCARE)](https://www.asa.org.uk/rulings/person-s--unknown-a24-1245836-person-s--unknown.html)
- ASA requires **genuine, evidenced testimonials**; advertisers unable to prove testimonials were genuine breached the CAP Code. — WebSearch synthesis of ASA rulings
- P&G (Always Discreet) was rapped by the ASA for misleading comparative incontinence-pad claims / on-screen text implying unsupported survey results. — [The Grocer](https://www.thegrocer.co.uk/marketing/procter-and-gamble-rapped-by-the-asa-for-misleading-incontinence-ad/685818.article)

### Inferences
- In the UK, keep claims to physical/absorbency performance (apparel positioning) and avoid medicinal "treats/relieves incontinence" language unless registered as a device with MHRA; substantiate comparative and statistical claims; ensure testimonials are genuine and documented. EU member states layer their own medical-device/claims rules (MDR) and consumer-protection law — stricter than US TikTok rows per the platform matrix.

### Gaps
- No ASA/CAP ruling specifically on *period/leak-proof* underwear was found; the VCARE and Always Discreet cases are the nearest analogues. UKRN-specific guidance was not located in this research pass.
