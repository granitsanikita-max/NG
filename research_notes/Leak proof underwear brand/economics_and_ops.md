# Unit Economics, Sizing/Fit, Returns & Hygiene-Logistics for a New Leak-Proof Underwear DTC Brand (2026)

Scope note: This covers the operational and financial reality of selling intimate apparel DTC, focused on a small new brand (not a scaled incumbent like Knix/Thinx). Most category-specific cost and benchmark data comes from vendor/manufacturer blogs and returns-platform content; independent, neutral data on underwear-specific COGS and return rates is thin, and this is flagged throughout. Figures that are my own modeling (not sourced) are labeled **[ESTIMATE]**.

## Sizing & Fit: why fit is the #1 return driver and how to reduce it

### Takeaway
Fit/sizing is the single largest cause of apparel returns (commonly cited as ~50% of apparel returns, up to ~70% when "fit or style" are combined), and fit-recommendation tools (fit quizzes, smart size charts) are the highest-leverage mitigation, with vendors reporting 15–50% reductions in returns on covered SKUs. Plus-size/extended-size is the hardest to fit and the thinnest-documented segment.

### Cited Findings
- Fit and sizing is "the single largest cause of returns," responsible for ~50% of apparel returns — [Richpanel](https://www.richpanel.com/learn/ecommerce-return-rates).
- Vendor sites cite a McKinsey figure that ~70% of apparel returns trace back to poor fit or style (not independently verified in this research) — [reported via WebSearch of fit-tool vendors].
- Bold Metrics' ML "Smart Size Chart" is marketed as reducing returns "by 50% or more," using 4–6 measurement inputs instead of tape measures — [Retail Dive](https://www.retaildive.com/news/bold-metrics-debuts-smart-size-chart-to-reduce-online-returns/549938).
- Menswear brand "Under 5'10" reports a 30% decrease in return rate for shoppers who bought the quiz-recommended size; its quiz asks usual pant size, fit preference, height, and seat/hip shape and recommends both size and inseam — [Easysize case study](https://www.easysize.me/case-studies/under-510).
- Easysize reports a 15–30% drop in returns on SKUs actively covered by a fit-recommendation tool, across ~450 brands (vendor internal figure) — [Easysize Fit Confidence Report](https://www.easysize.me/fit-confidence-report).
- Design best practices that recur: keep the quiz short (one guide recommends ~4 questions max to protect completion rate); place the recommendation on the product detail page at the moment of decision plus a dedicated fit-finder page; map recommendations to the brand's own size chart rather than generic charts — [Fudge](https://www.fudge.ai/features/fit-size-quiz/); [Shopify Fit Quiz app](https://apps.shopify.com/fit-quiz); [Fit Finder app](https://apps.shopify.com/size-fit-app).
- Some fit apps deliberately avoid sensitive inputs ("no weight, body measurements, body scans"); note this may conflict with accuracy for plus-size shoppers, so it is a tradeoff to test — [Shopify Fit Quiz app](https://apps.shopify.com/fit-quiz).
- Bras specifically are "harder to fit" and warrant free, flexible exchanges plus optional personal fit consultations — [Loop Returns](https://loopreturns.com/blog/how-to-build-a-return-policy-for-intimates-and-bathing-suit-brands/).

### Inferences
- A fit quiz is effectively mandatory, not optional, for an underwear brand: fit is the dominant return reason, underwear is often non-returnable (so a wrong-size first purchase = a dead customer, not a cheap exchange), and tools claim 15–50% return reduction — the ROI is large relative to the ~$5–20/mo Shopify-app cost.
- "Size up" guidance and generous rise/thigh/curve descriptions matter most for plus-size, where published fit-tool efficacy data is absent — treat plus-size fit as the highest-risk SKU block and over-invest in fit content, model diversity, and per-SKU measurement tables.
- Best-practice stack for a new brand: (1) detailed per-SKU size chart with body measurements AND garment measurements; (2) a 3–5 question fit quiz on the PDP; (3) explicit "between sizes? size up" copy; (4) extended size range from launch (don't bolt on plus later); (5) fit/customer-measurement data captured to refine size recs over time.

### Gaps
- No independent (non-vendor) measurement of fit-tool return reduction.
- No underwear- or plus-size-specific fit-return efficacy data; all case studies are for other apparel categories.

## Returns & Hygiene: can underwear be returned, what brands do, and what return rate to assume

### Takeaway
Underwear is commonly sold "final sale" for hygiene reasons, but the better-converting playbook for intimates is exchange-only / one-time free exchange / keep-it rather than strict final sale, because strict no-return policies push dissatisfied buyers toward chargebacks. Returned intimates frequently cannot be resold (legal/hygiene), so most intimate returns are a near-total loss. Assume an apparel return rate of ~20–40% directionally, but underwear's effective return rate is held down structurally by final-sale/exchange-only policies (no clean underwear-specific figure was found).

### Cited Findings
- Many brands classify underwear as "final sale" — cannot be returned or exchanged — explicitly for hygiene reasons — [WeSupply Labs](https://wesupplylabs.com/return-policy-for-intimates-and-bathing-suit-tips-for-brands/); [Loop Returns](https://loopreturns.com/blog/how-to-build-a-return-policy-for-intimates-and-bathing-suit-brands/).
- Boody excludes opened Intimates (bodysuits, socks, underwear) from returns unless faulty, but allows bra returns/exchanges if policy met — [Boody](https://boody.co.uk/pages/returns).
- For swimwear/lingerie, several policies require an intact protective hygiene liner; if removed, the return is rejected (example: Summersalt swimwear requires original tags and hygienic liner in place) — [Loop Returns](https://loopreturns.com/blog/how-to-build-a-return-policy-for-intimates-and-bathing-suit-brands/).
- For swimwear and intimates, returned items "frequently cannot be resold for legal and hygiene reasons," making each return a complete loss — [WeSupply Labs](https://wesupplylabs.com/return-policy-for-intimates-and-bathing-suit-tips-for-brands/).
- Loop's recommended structure: if underwear is a small share of sales, make it final sale; if it is a top seller, build returns/exchanges in. Even under a no-return default, offer first-time buyers one free exchange / keep-the-item — framed as a customer-acquisition cost that also yields fit-failure data — [Loop Returns](https://loopreturns.com/blog/how-to-build-a-return-policy-for-intimates-and-bathing-suit-brands/).
- Policies must state hygiene standards explicitly (what condition qualifies, keep own underwear on when trying on, shower before/after) — [Loop Returns](https://loopreturns.com/blog/how-to-build-a-return-policy-for-intimates-and-bathing-suit-brands/).
- Incumbent policies (confirm on brand sites; sourced via reviews/blogs, not primary): Knix reportedly allows returns/exchanges on underwear within 30 days (single blog); Thinx reportedly offers exchange or refund to original payment within 60 days — [The Frugal Girl](https://thefrugalgirl.com/a-review-of-thinx-and-knix-from-a-household-of-ladies). Knix customer reviews describe exchange-only / partial-credit outcomes for final-sale items — [reviews.io](https://www.reviews.io/company-reviews/store/knixwear).
- Return reasons: fit/sizing ~50% of apparel returns; bracketing (buying multiple sizes/variants intending to return most) is now practiced by a majority of online shoppers (up from ~40% in 2018) — [Richpanel](https://www.richpanel.com/learn/ecommerce-return-rates).
- Return rates: overall ecommerce ~19.3–20% (NRF ~19.3% online; blended US retail 15.8%); apparel 20–40%, "closer to 30–40%" per Richpanel intro; a 2023 Coresight figure put online apparel at 24.4% — [Richpanel](https://www.richpanel.com/learn/ecommerce-return-rates); [WebSearch of NRF-citing benchmarks]. UK clothing ~23.6% of ecommerce orders in 2025 (ZigZag) — [WebSearch].
- Reverse-logistics processing cost is quantified only for other categories ($30–65/item for electronics; furniture reverse logistics can exceed product margin); support labor is a "major hidden cost" but unquantified — [Richpanel](https://www.richpanel.com/learn/ecommerce-return-rates).
- Chargeback/dispute risk: strict no-return policies push unhappy buyers to dispute rather than ask for a refund (disputing is effortless; contacting the merchant "feels like a hassle"), removing the merchant's chance to resolve first — [Chargeflow via WebSearch]. First-party ("friendly") fraud is ~21% of chargebacks under Mastercard's 2025 conservative definition, up to ~70% under wider definitions — [Riskified](https://www.riskified.com/learning/chargebacks/friendly-fraud/); [Javelin](https://www.javelinstrategy.com/research/merchant-chargebacks-are-rise-due-friendly-fraud). There are no explicit laws protecting merchants the way FCBA/Reg E protect consumers, so burden of proof falls on the merchant — [WebSearch].

### Inferences
- **Recommended policy for a new leak-proof brand:** default to exchange-only within 30 days with an intact hygiene liner/seal requirement, plus a one-time "fit guarantee" (keep-or-reship the correct size on the first order). This captures Loop's acquisition logic, defuses chargeback risk from strict final sale, and limits cash-refund leakage. Reserve strict "final sale" only for clearance/last-chance SKUs.
- **Restock vs destroy:** model opened/worn intimate returns as ~100% loss (not resellable) — the return cost = full COGS + return shipping + processing labor, with zero salvage. Only unopened, liner-intact returns can be restocked. This makes reducing returns (fit quiz) far more valuable than optimizing reverse logistics.
- **Return-rate assumption for modeling [ESTIMATE]:** because underwear policy structurally suppresses returns, assume an *effective processed return/exchange rate* of ~8–15% for a new brand with exchange-only + liner rules and a fit quiz, vs the 20–40% seen in openly-returnable apparel. Budget each return/exchange at ~full-COGS loss + ~$8–12 reverse shipping/processing. Flag this as an estimate — no underwear-specific published rate was found.

### Gaps
- No underwear/lingerie-specific published return rate (Richpanel and others do not break it out).
- No published restock-vs-destroy cost figures for intimates (only electronics/furniture reverse-logistics costs).
- Primary/current Knix, Thinx, Everdries policy pages were not retrievable in this research — incumbent policy details are from blogs/reviews and must be confirmed on the brands' own sites.
- No data directly testing whether final-sale vs exchange-only changes a brand's chargeback rate.

## Unit Economics: COGS, price points, AOV, bundles, and margin after returns/shipping/CAC

### Takeaway
Leak-proof underwear lands at roughly $5–12/pair COGS (ex-works ~$6–9 at 1,000 units + $1.50–3 freight/duty), retails ~$20–50/pair (incumbents), and is sold heavily in 3-packs at ~$60–81 to lift AOV. A new brand needs a high gross margin (target ~70–80% / ~4–6x markup) to survive returns + ~15–20% shipping/fulfillment + Meta CAC. Example model below shows thin-but-positive contribution only if AOV is pushed to a multi-pack.

### Cited Findings (all from one manufacturer's blog unless noted — vendor estimates, not independent)
- Ex-factory wholesale cost ~$3.70–12.00/pair depending on construction/absorbency/volume; ~$6–9/pair ex-works China at 1,000 units — [Ljvogues](https://www.ljvogues.com/period-underwear-manufacturing-costs.html).
- Add ~$1.50–3.00/unit freight + duties → landed roughly ~$7.50–12/pair at low volume — [Ljvogues](https://www.ljvogues.com/period-underwear-manufacturing-costs.html).
- A landed cost below ~$5 "should be questioned" (likely excludes leak-proof durability or compliance testing) — [Ljvogues](https://www.ljvogues.com/period-underwear-manufacturing-costs.html).
- Cost composition: main body fabric 20–30% of cost; absorbent core 20–30% of material cost; TPU leak-proof layer 15–25% of material cost; gusset is 3–4 distinct layers. Light→super/overnight absorbency raises core cost 30–40%; medical-grade TPU vs standard PUL +15–25%; advanced leak-proof finishing vs basic stitching raises assembly 30–50%; custom OEM vs ODM white-label +10–40% — [Ljvogues](https://www.ljvogues.com/period-underwear-manufacturing-costs.html).
- Alibaba listings reportedly $2.60–5.00/piece but these are starting prices excluding design, testing, packaging, inspection, freight — [Ljvogues via WebSearch].
- Retail: established brands sell $20–50/pair (framed as 4–6x markup over factory cost); a separate sourcing article cites premium "clean" brands $25–40 and compliance-focused $30–55 — [Ljvogues](https://www.ljvogues.com/period-underwear-cost-breakdown-wholesale-price). An independent science-fair abstract notes period underwear retails $20–40/pair — [Society for Science abstract](https://abstracts.societyforscience.org/Home/PrintPdf/24390).
- Bundle/AOV reality (incumbents): Knixteen Super Leakproof No-Show Bikini 3-Pack listed $81.00 with 15% off at checkout (~$68.85) — [Knixteen](https://www.knixteen.com/products/super-leakproof-no-show-bikini-3-pack). Thinx promos commonly "up to 20% off when you buy 3+" — [Promocodes](https://www.promocodes.com/thinx). Single Thinx briefs ~$19.99–40 at retail/marketplace; Hanes (mass-market, non-premium) 3-pack seen at $9.79 — [WebSearch]. Knix warehouse-sale (one-off) offered a leakproof trio for $27 — [Fashion Magazine](https://fashionmagazine.com/lifestyle/knix-warehouse-sale/).
- Platform-wide DTC context: Triple Whale 2025 (~35,000 accounts) median AOV $71.69, median ROAS 1.86, platform CPM $14.19 (+~20% YoY), platform CPA $38.19; apparel CVR median 1.46% — [Triple Whale via WebSearch] (direct page 403'd).

### Inferences / Example Model [ESTIMATE — illustrative, built on sourced ranges]
Assume a small new brand, single-pair "hero" price $32, landed COGS $9/pair, and a 3-pack at $78 (landed COGS $27).

Per-3-pack order (target AOV play):
- Revenue: $78
- COGS: $27 (35% of revenue) → gross margin $51 (65%)
- Payment processing (~3%): ~$2.30
- Outbound ship + pick/pack + packaging: ~$9 (soft goods, light)
- Effective returns/exchanges reserve at ~12% of orders, each ~full-pack-COGS + $10 reverse cost ≈ $37 loss → ~$4.40/order amortized
- Contribution before CAC: ~$78 − $27 − $2.30 − $9 − $4.40 ≈ **$35.30 (~45% of revenue)**
- To run Meta profitably on first order alone, blended CAC must be < ~$35; at CPM $14 and apparel CVR ~1.5% plus funnel drop-off, a realistic new-brand blended CAC of $30–60+ means the **first order is often break-even-to-negative — the brand must earn its margin on the 2nd+ order (LTV)**.

Single-pair order ($32): gross margin ~$23 (72%); after processing/ship/returns reserve, contribution ~$10–13 — almost always **below CAC**, so single-pair-only acquisition loses money. This is the core reason the category is sold in bundles.

Rules of thumb:
- Target **~70–80% gross margin / 4–6x markup** so there is headroom for ~15–20% fulfillment+shipping, ~10–15% returns/exchange loss, and Meta CAC.
- **AOV must clear ~2.3–2.5x the pair price via bundles** for first-order economics to approach break-even against DTC CAC.

### Gaps
- No independent COGS breakdown; all cost figures are from one manufacturer's blog + Alibaba listings.
- Current primary retail prices from Thinx/Knix/Everdries own sites were not retrievable; bundle prices are from a teen sub-brand and coupon aggregators.
- No published category-specific fulfillment/pick-pack cost for underwear (used general soft-goods estimate).

## Backend / LTV: subscription, auto-replenish, repeat behavior, email/SMS

### Takeaway
Underwear is a "basics" replenishable, which is a structurally stronger subscription/LTV category than occasion apparel; replenishment subscriptions are reported to lift LTV ~2–5x, and because first-order DTC economics are thin, repeat purchase (not the first sale) is where the money is. But apparel repeat rates are only ~25–35%, so retention must be engineered.

### Cited Findings
- "Basics" brands (underwear, t-shirts, activewear) show higher purchase frequency and stronger LTV than occasion wear; apparel repeat rate ~25–35% (i.e., most first-time buyers don't return) — [LTV.ai](https://ltv.ai/blog/average-customer-ltv-ecommerce-vertical-benchmarks).
- Subscriber premium estimates: one source puts a subscriber at 3–5x a one-time buyer at equal gross margin (lift almost entirely from repeat frequency); another (beauty) cites ~2x LTV gap subscription vs non-subscription — [eightx.co](https://eightx.co/blog/average-subscriber-lifetime-and-ltv-multiple-by-category).
- Apparel/fashion median 24-month LTV ~$220 (top quartile ~$320); subscription/recurring ~$580 median — [TopGrowthMarketing LTV calculator](https://topgrowthmarketing.com/tools/ltv-calculator/).
- ~45% of subscribers stay subscribed ≥1 year; apparel/home brands need repeat-purchase quality to reach a healthy LTV:CAC of ~3:1; LTV must be computed on gross profit, not revenue — [Shopify/OrderGroove](https://www.shopify.com/resources/retention/ordergroove).
- Underwear replenishment subscriptions have real precedent (e.g., Freshpair launched auto-ship of underwear/bras/socks on OrderGroove's platform) — [Digital Commerce 360](https://www.digitalcommerce360.com/2012/02/13/freshpaircom-launches-freshpair-subscriptions/).

### Inferences
- Auto-replenish is viable but underwear is *durable* — replenishment cadence is long (quarters, not monthly), so position subscription as "restock every 2–3 months / add a new color" rather than consumable monthly. Use subscribe-and-save discounts (~10–15%) plus new-color drops to justify recurring value.
- Because first-order contribution is thin vs CAC, the business case rests on getting cohort repeat rate above the ~25–35% apparel baseline via subscription + email/SMS; target LTV:CAC ≥ 3:1 on a gross-profit basis.
- Email/SMS (implied best practice): post-purchase fit follow-up (reduces returns and seeds exchanges), replenishment reminders timed to ~60–90 days, and bundle/color cross-sell are the highest-value flows for a basics brand.

### Gaps
- No underwear-specific subscription churn/retention curve; multiples are cross-category vendor estimates with undisclosed methodology.
- No data on optimal replenishment cadence for leak-proof underwear specifically.

## CAC Reality: CPM/CPA benchmarks and margin headroom

### Takeaway
Meta costs have risen (platform CPM ~$14, +~20% YoY; platform CPA ~$38) while apparel conversion is low (~1.5% median), so new-brand blended CAC frequently lands in the $30–60+ range — often at or above first-order contribution. The category only works with high gross margin, bundle-driven AOV, and LTV from repeat/subscription.

### Cited Findings
- Triple Whale 2025 (~35,000 accounts): platform CPM $14.19 (+~20% YoY); platform CPA $38.19 (+~1%); median ROAS 1.86; median AOV $71.69; apparel CVR median 1.46% (+9.4% YoY). An "Apparel & Accessories" CPA figure of $36.76 appears but the table layout is ambiguous — [Triple Whale via WebSearch] (page returned 403 on direct fetch).
- TrendTrack 2025: apparel averaged CPC $0.45, CTR 1.24%, CVR 4.11%, CPA $10.98, ROAS 2.30:1 — much lower CPA than Triple Whale, likely due to different attribution/conversion definitions — [TrendTrack](https://www.trendtrack.io/blog-post/meta-ad-spend-by-industry).
- Typical DTC apparel ROAS ~1.5–2.5 — [WebSearch of multiple benchmarks].
- Facebook Ads benchmark aggregators (2026) corroborate rising CPM/CPC and single-digit-percent CVR for retail/apparel — [Influee](https://influee.co/blog/facebook-ads-benchmarks).

### Inferences
- The CPA spread ($11 TrendTrack vs ~$37–38 Triple Whale) reflects attribution window/definition differences — a new brand should model break-even CAC off its own AOV × gross margin, not off a vendor CPA. With ROAS ~1.8–2.3 and AOV ~$70, implied CAC is ~$30–40, consistent with the single-pair order losing money and the 3-pack being roughly first-order break-even.
- **Margin headroom needed [ESTIMATE]:** to run Meta profitably, target gross margin ~70–80% and AOV ≥ ~$70 (bundle), so that a $30–40 CAC leaves positive first-order contribution; below ~65% margin or ~$50 AOV, acquisition is subsidized and must be recovered on repeat/subscription within the first 1–2 reorders.
- Expect CAC to rise over 2026 (CPM trending +~20% YoY); build the model with CAC stress-tested to $50–60 and verify LTV:CAC still clears ~3:1.

### Gaps
- No leak-proof/intimate-apparel-specific CPM/CPA (benchmarks are category "apparel" at best).
- Conflicting CPA figures across vendors with undisclosed attribution methods; treat all as directional.
- Triple Whale's apparel-specific CPM was not isolated in results; direct source page blocked (403).
